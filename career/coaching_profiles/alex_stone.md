# Alex Stone: coaching profile

Jacksonville Jaguars head coach. Age 50. This portrait looks back from February 2, 2014, after his first Jacksonville season. Descriptions of temperament and comfort are interpretations of his recorded work and conversations; the linked records supply the events.

The [complete NFL Coach Sheet](alex_stone_coach_sheet.md) expands the early-2014 portrait. Dated updates below preserve subsequent evidence. His [training-camp approach](#how-he-wants-training-camp-run) records the user's explicit direction separately from demonstrated coaching results.

Stone is an offensive head coach who likes to make the defense declare itself, then move the same players into a different problem. He is comfortable with the play sheet. He likes having the final offensive decision. His offense gives tight ends, backs and movable receivers several ways to do a familiar job, and he spends considerable preparation time making those jobs fit together. He also wants a physical running game, with a fullback, a pulling guard or another tight end doing something useful at the point of attack.

The first Jacksonville season has exposed a less tidy version of that coach. He can see a passing answer and keep pursuing it while a useful running game goes unused. He can protect a young quarterback by taking something off the sheet, then leave it off long after the original problem should have been revisited. His teaching is most complete when he has time to show the correction, run it again and change the look. During the season, that same correction can shrink to a sentence while everybody moves on to the next opponent.

He has become more specific about his own mistakes. He has not become uniformly better at preventing them.

## What he brought with him

Stone arrived in Jacksonville with substantial experience of calling games and running a team. He had been a primary caller in college, taken over the New Orleans offensive calls during 2011, and kept them through a full season as interim head coach in 2012. Jacksonville was his first permanent NFL head-coaching job, but Sunday responsibility was already familiar. His 2013 development begins there, rather than with a novice discovering whether he can speak into a headset.

His background helps explain the football he chooses. Ten seasons playing tight end were followed by work in quarterback preparation, the New England secondary, tight ends and passing-game coordination, then New Orleans offensive coordination. His experience includes both what a receiver is trying to do and how the defense distributes him. In his current offense, the alignment can change while the route combination stays recognizable to the quarterback. A tight end can block, chip and release, detach or occupy the space that another formation gives to a back. Those are recurring football interests, visible in the book and the weekly plans.

The important difference from New Orleans is the group he is responsible for building. There he had worked with Drew Brees, an established offense and much of an inherited staff. In Jacksonville he selected the coaches, installed his own program and lived with the consequences of choosing and developing Kirk Cousins. By January 2014, he has a season's knowledge of how these particular people work together. He knows more about where his instructions fail them, too. ## The offense he wants to call

Stone's preferred variety comes from presentation and sequence. Against St. Louis, he added a small six-lineman package, shifted two-tight-end personnel into Empty without substituting, and carried a Jet Sweep and a tight-end screen. The core offense stayed in place. The preparation question was whether the same people could make the defense honor different answers. Against San Diego, Kelce's split-flow blocking led into a leak off that action. In London, Thielen moved among slot, stack and motion assignments while Blackmon returned to a narrower outside job.

That gives Stone a recognizable offensive style: substantial bodies on the field, a credible downhill run, quick access throws, and opportunities to throw behind a defender who has committed to something else. He likes a protected shot with a reason. He will spread the formation, but he wants the blocking and protection answers to survive the change. He would rather change who performs a known job than ask the whole offense to learn another unrelated play.

His preparation is hands-on. In the opener's week, Thursday paired runs with their complements and included Stone calling a mock game from the sheet. Friday removed calls that had not been clean. By the first Tennessee week, he and Tice attached protection to every pass concept before it went into the plan. That is where his confidence is easiest to see: he knows what he wants to carry, what information the quarterback needs and what work he expects before he will call it.

He gives the quarterback defined room to act. The book has paired checks, access throws and protection answers. Cousins can tell him on Thursday which calls he trusts before Friday's final selection. Stone listens to that discussion and keeps the final decision. He is comfortable with a quarterback who can argue a football point; he still expects to decide what the offense takes into the game. ## What the season changed in his calling

Houston is the clearest example of Stone's passing instinct getting ahead of his plan. He went into the first meeting intending to make Houston stop the run. Jacksonville then finished with seventeen runs and forty-five dropbacks. His subsequent explanation was specific: Houston had not stopped the running game; he had stopped calling it. The problem was already visible before the late deficit made passing more necessary.

His response tells something about how he works. Before Cleveland he added a question after three consecutive called passes: why is he still throwing? Being behind and feeling that something has to happen would not be enough. He did not impose an automatic run on the next snap. He wanted a check on his own reasoning while preserving his freedom to call the next pass.

There is some change here, and some persistence. The run stayed involved in the Houston rematch. The check remained in subsequent plans. Cousins's exit interview still put six consecutive passes at Cleveland on Stone's review sheet. A longer passing sequence does not itself prove the next call was wrong, but recording the question had not settled whether he was using it well. He enters 2014 aware of a particular tendency: he can become absorbed in the answer he thinks the passing game ought to provide.

His caution produces a different problem. Boot Flood came off the primary sheet after the early interception and sacks and never returned. In January, Cousins asked whether it had remained out because the play was wrong or because leaving it out had become easier. Stone acknowledged that the staff had not preserved an adequate account of whether the original trouble belonged to the read, protection or call. He agreed to revisit it; he did not restore it in the interview.

Stone therefore finished the year both willing to keep calling passes and increasingly restrictive about which passes he would carry. Those tendencies can coexist. He trusts his calling more readily than he trusts every available answer with this offense. The next step is to distinguish a necessary restriction from one that has outlived its purpose. That review is still open. ## What it is like to be coached by him

Stone gives a player a job he can describe. In spring, the staff explains it, shows it, walks it, runs it, corrects it and returns to it with something changed. He wants to find out whether the player can recognize the answer without a coach supplying it. His motor-learning background is relevant to that method, but the useful evidence is in what the players retained. Thielen described the move into a larger receiver role in those terms: the concepts stayed familiar while his location and responsibility changed.

There is patience in Stone's personnel decisions. Lane Johnson struggled early and stayed at right tackle with structural help. When Meester was unavailable, Stone put Brewster at center rather than rearranging several positions; Brewster subsequently kept the starting job after Meester returned. Thielen's place did not disappear when Blackmon became available. Stone can change the support around a player while leaving him responsible for the work. He can also let a younger player's performance alter the established order.

His patience is more convincing when it comes with an answer the player can use. The exit interviews expose occasions when it did not. Kelce kept hearing that his attached blocking needed correction without a clear account of what would close that issue. Thielen's individual development period disappeared after the bye, and nobody told him whether the work was complete or merely postponed. Shorts got extensive game preparation but less of the receiver-quarterback work against changing coverage that had helped in spring. Protection took time, and other work lost its place.

Stone's way of speaking can make that worse. He compresses a coaching point into a short instruction and expects it to remain useful through the week. Cousins understood the instruction about abandoning a shot when its trigger disappeared; he wanted the matching clips, including one he had played correctly. Shorts likewise wanted to see the snap. Their complaint was about the missing explanation and comparison, not a wish to escape correction.

The January conversations show Stone willing to hear that. Cousins disputed the idea that every interception should be filed as a bad quarterback decision. Stone invited the argument and brought the receiver and protection coaches into the review. He did not require Cousins to concede the disputed Cleveland throw before they watched it. When Thielen raised two coaches correcting the same rep, Stone clarified who should speak. When Shorts said he had learned of a changed emphasis from his targets rather than the meeting, Stone told Drake to explain those changes during installation.

That is the most useful reading of his manner: direct, particular and willing to discuss the work, with a tendency to assume an instruction has communicated more than the player actually received. He will acknowledge a teaching failure in the room. Whether the promised film and follow-up reach the player is a separate weakness the season has made harder to overlook. ## How he runs the rest of the team

Stone delegates substantial football authority. Tice runs the weekday offensive process. Crennel prepares and calls the defense. Stone gives defensive priorities, such as fitting the back before chasing pressure or accounting for a particular receiver, and leaves Crennel to call the unit. His New England defensive experience lets him question a coverage or discuss an adjustment with Crennel.

His relationship with Caldwell also has a defined limit. He sought stronger influence over quarterback commitments during the original negotiation, accepted consultation with Caldwell retaining the final decision, and worked within that arrangement through the season. He supplies football projections and personnel recommendations. He does not promise players contracts he cannot grant. After the season, he asked for no new authority or contract terms. That is a coach willing to work with a general manager, while continuing to press a detailed view of what the team should become.

Around players, he keeps some boundaries firm. He can maintain a football decision that is questioned, as he did with Blackmon's additional inactive weeks. Medical clearance remains with the medical staff. The spring family dinners included brief remarks and no football instruction or evaluation. Those events show an effort to make room for players' lives without treating access to them as another coaching resource. They do not establish whether he is privately warm, funny, remote or sociable.

Lowry's departure now tests the division of work. Stone is covering special teams while retaining the offense and the head-coach role. He has selected Bobby April as the replacement target; no appointment has closed. The supported conclusion is that Stone can oversee specialists and coordinators and knows he needs the vacancy filled. There is little basis for describing technical special-teams work as another equally comfortable specialty. ## How he wants training camp run

**User-defined approach, September 30, 2026; applies to the current 2014 camp and carries forward unless changed.** This describes Stone's intended method. The [camp plan](../2014/04_Training_Camp_and_Preseason/Training_Camp/staff_plan.md#stones-camp-standard) makes it operational; dated practice and game records establish what he and the staff actually did. It adds no practice, test result or role decision to the July 29 history.

Stone wants camp to put the spring teaching under pressure. Players have learned the calls and walked through the answers. Now he wants to see the guard get to his block, the tight end hold his assignment, the back protect, the defender get off a block and the whole unit handle the next snap. He wants a physical team, and he expects to spend the permitted padded periods finding out whether its technique survives resistance and repeated football work. The staff specifies the contact and finish for each drill; quarterbacks are protected, and a whistle ends the rep.

Everybody reports on the applicable reporting date. An established starter has the same obligation to arrive prepared as a rookie. Veteran status provides no blanket exemption from camp or its physical standards. Stone includes himself and his assistants: coaches must meet a written fitness standard suited to their work, as players meet standards suited to their positions. Medical clearance and individual limitations remain with the clinicians. A coach's age, health and duties affect the test he is given; his title does not excuse him from having one. The performance staff defines the test and passing standard beforehand, then provides a plan and retest when someone falls short.

Maximum participation means being prepared for, and taking part in, the work assigned that day. A healthy player works the relevant individual, group, team, situational and special-teams periods. A player receiving treatment follows his permitted work and remains involved in meetings and observation where medically appropriate. An individualized recovery day must have a football or medical reason; it is not a standing privilege attached to seniority. Stone wants players available for useful work across camp, and expects the coaches to plan the workload accordingly.

He mixes groups to learn something. A reserve guard needs an opportunity beside the regular center. A young receiver needs more than work against the same reserve corner. The defense has to make its calls with its usual communicator out of the drill. Starters also have to function beside substitutes. Everyone receives meaningful work in a taught, suitable role, including opportunities against established players when appropriate. The staff still needs repetitions with the likely starting combinations. Reps follow the question being tested, and a trial with the first group does not itself award a job.

Stone practices situations as problems the players must solve: getting out from near their goal line, protecting a lead, needing a score, making a substitution with the clock running, or losing the usual caller for a series. He looks for players who understand what the situation asks of the call and can act without the sideline talking them through it. He also looks at his coaches: whether their instructions agree, whether they corrected the right problem, and whether they returned to the player after promising another look.

His assessment starts with the job in his system. A receiver's value includes the release and spacing that make another route work. A tight end who can move around still has to perform the attached assignment he is given. A fast edge rusher must keep the lane or execute the exchange the call requires. Stone wants to know which combinations let him run the football he intends, what support they need, and what changes when an opponent takes away the first answer. His preference for physical, adaptable players does not predetermine who meets that description.

## The difference entering his second Jacksonville season

At hiring, Stone could describe a program and explain why its parts should work together. He now has specific people who can tell him where that description matched their experience and where it did not. Cousins can operate the offense and challenge the way he is being protected. Thielen can explain what helped him change jobs and when that help stopped. Kelce can ask why a correction remained open for an entire season. These are relationships with a shared working history, including unresolved disagreements.

Stone has not lost his interest in formations, combinations or offensive control. His autumn plans still added wrinkles, and he retained the calls through the postseason. The change is that his review now has to include the cost of his preferences. Keeping a tight end in protection removes a receiver. Giving protection the largest practice block takes time from something else. Removing an unreliable call makes the sheet cleaner and may leave fewer answers for a familiar opponent. He has begun naming those costs in discussion instead of treating a well-organized plan as a sufficient explanation.

The fullest confidence remains in preparing and calling offense. Building his own staff and making earned-role decisions are now part of his Jacksonville experience. Sustaining individual teaching through the back half of a season is less settled, and the playoff ending does not establish reliable late-game management. His immediate offseason work includes his own calls alongside the players' mistakes. Boot Flood remains under review. Kelce still needs the promised blocking assessment and a clearer account of the receiving opportunities the staff did and did not give him.
