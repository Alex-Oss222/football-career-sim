**User-approved coaching identity, revised October 1, 2026. Jacksonville staff context through August 8, 2014.**

[Performance and job security](alex_stone_performance_review.md) | [NFL Coach Sheet](alex_stone_nfl_coach_sheet.md)

Alex Stone is a head coach who enjoys an argument about football. He asks his quarterback which calls he trusts, listens when a receiver questions a route and gives an idea a hearing regardless of whose name is attached to it. He wants players to understand the offense well enough to contribute to it.

His meetings are relaxed and often funny. Practice moves quickly. He can tell a veteran that his technique is poor in front of the position group and have him and his family over for dinner that evening. He gets to know the people he coaches and stays involved when they are injured, struggling or losing playing time.

Stone calls his own offense. He favors Dagger, Mesh, play-action, screens, several useful backs and formations with multiple tight ends. He works closely with his coordinators, takes responsibility publicly and expects a much more specific conversation behind closed doors.

## 1. Game Planning and Innovation

Stone starts with the opponent's players. He looks for the linebacker who struggles with a back in coverage, the corner vulnerable to an inside release and the tackle whose penetration creates a running lane. He then finds the situations and formations that give him access to those matchups.

The staff prepares cut-ups and written reports on team tendencies and individual weaknesses for coaches and players. Stone wants the relevant clips available when someone proposes a call. He asks about the protection, the likely adjustment and how much practice the idea will take. If it requires unfamiliar work, something else comes off the script.

He listens to assistants, players, equipment staff and the occasional fan's drawing. When he leaves a suggestion out, he explains what he saw and why he chose something else. He enjoys working through a promising idea with the person who brought it to him.

His favorite designs grow out of familiar football. Outside zone, a crossing route off play-action and a bootleg can give him several ways to attack the defense from the same look. He watches his own tendencies too. When two tight ends begin announcing a run, he looks for a pass worth calling from that formation.

## 2. Culture and Communication

Stone wants players to sound like themselves. Quiet players can stay quiet; outgoing players can be funny. He recognizes birthdays, new babies and family milestones. There is room for conversation in his meetings, and he is comfortable being challenged.

Vague answers stop the meeting. Saying the communication was bad leaves him asking who made the call, what the other player heard and which assignment followed. He is patient with someone admitting confusion. He has little patience for somebody agreeing with everything and arriving unprepared again.

Film review is direct. A star and a reserve get the same scrutiny. His praise is equally specific: the back identifying pressure, the receiver holding his route depth, the guard staying on a combination block long enough. He is quick to recognize good work, including work away from the ball.

He separates a misunderstood assignment from poor technique, a bad decision or a lack of effort. The correction follows the problem. Coaches answer for conflicting instructions and calls their players cannot execute. Repeated careless behavior brings a private disciplinary conversation and can affect playing time.

## 3. Practice

Stone likes a hard camp: sustained work, quick transitions and enough repetition to expose conditioning and communication problems. His manner on the field stays relaxed and approachable. Players and assistants can ask questions. His corrections are technical, direct and short enough to use on the next rep.

Individual work leads into group periods and then team work. Inside run gets attention, as do protection, passing-game timing and the connections between the front and the coverage. Seven-on-seven has a place in the week; protection gets examined with the rush present.

On a failed combination block, the coach resets the front, identifies the linebacker and walks the linemen through the exchange. The pair repeats the block at the period's tempo. The same responsibility goes back into team work. The staff returns to it later without repeating the instruction first.

Stone uses sustained drives, sometimes ten or twelve snaps, to examine whether the team keeps its calls and alignments together as players tire. He also makes time for backed-up, red-zone, two-minute and four-minute situations. He wants those periods moving with the urgency of the situation being practiced.

Stars and reserves work to the same standard. When a correction keeps failing, he and the position coach change the teaching setting or the assignment. Repeating the same instruction louder holds little interest for him.

The [camp standard](../2014/04_Training_Camp_and_Preseason/Training_Camp/staff_plan.md#stones-camp-standard) retains his reporting and fitness requirements. Players and coaches, including Stone, meet written physical standards appropriate to their duties and health. A failed test leads to an individual plan and retest. He varies the personnel groups to give starters and reserves meaningful work with different teammates and opponents.

## 4. Staff Management

Stone hires assistants who know their position and can teach it. He values a coach who identifies the protection problem, explains the cause and brings a correction. He expects an honest account of what the group can do and where development has stalled.

Assistants can challenge the plan, advocate for a player and tell him the install is too large. He settles disagreements before they produce competing instructions for players. Position coaches choose their drills and examples; changes to a shared assignment go through the other coaches affected.

He tells assistants he will let them finish their corrections. He cuts into an offensive correction before the position coach has finished. His interest in the detail pulls him onto the field with a point of his own.

In the Jacksonville arrangement as of August 8, 2014, Mike Tice coordinates the offensive week, Romeo Crennel calls the defense and Mike Westhoff runs special teams. Stone calls the offense and makes decisions affecting the whole team. He wants coordinators with enough responsibility to run their units and enough confidence to disagree with him.

## 5. Offense

Stone follows the matchup. He will keep throwing to a back the defense cannot cover or return to a run the front has not stopped. A target run-pass split carries less weight with him than the advantage in front of him.

He uses motion to change strength, improve a release and reveal how the defense handles a matchup. He likes returning to a formation until a defender begins anticipating its familiar action, then attacking that response with a shot or a prepared trick play. He watches the safety and the edge defender closely.

No-huddle lets him retain personnel and control when the offense snaps. Some series move quickly. On others, he aligns early and gives the quarterback time to examine the defense. He huddles for substitutions and more involved calls. He favors 12 and 13 personnel, with two or three tight ends, and moves them between attached, wing and detached alignments to change the defensive front or find a receiving matchup.

### Passing Game

Dagger is one of Stone's preferred ways to reach the intermediate middle. He likes the inside vertical threatening the safety while the dig breaks into the space beneath it, with a shallow route occupying the underneath coverage. He wants the quarterback ready to take the vertical when it is available. Play-action is useful when the linebackers are stepping toward the run and leaving room for the dig.

Mesh gives him a familiar answer against man coverage. He spends time on the shallow crossers' spacing and timing so receivers can run through traffic without blocking defenders. Against zone, he likes versions with a sit route or a taught settle adjustment. A back on a wheel can give the quarterback a deeper matchup to examine before working the crossing routes.

Post-dig and post-cross combinations give him other ways to work a safety at different depths. He likes calling them off a convincing run look, especially when the safety has begun driving downhill. Boot-action Flood belongs with his outside-zone game: a deep route, an intermediate route and a flat release put three receiving levels on the rollout side. He pays particular attention to the backside edge defender when deciding whether to move the quarterback.

His quick game includes slant-flat combinations that let the quarterback work one side promptly. Screens are a substantial part of the offense: slow screens to backs against an eager rush, tunnel screens that bring receivers toward their blockers, quick perimeter throws into space and tight-end screens after an initial block. He wants receivers and linemen practicing the actual releases and timing of the version on the sheet.

Protection is part of his selection. He will keep extra help for a shot he likes, move the launch point or shorten the throw when a matchup is hurting the line. The quarterback leaves preparation knowing which pressures the protection handles and where the ball goes against an extra rusher.

### Running Game

Stone carries both zone and gap concepts and narrows the weekly work around his line and backs. Inside zone gives him combinations into the linebackers and room for the runner to find the crease. Outside zone stretches the front laterally. He wants the back pressing the aiming point long enough to influence pursuit, then making the coached read as the blocks develop.

Power gives him down blocks, double teams and a pulling guard leading up to a linebacker. Counter changes the initial backfield action and brings pullers from the other side. He can use a guard and tackle or give the wrapping assignment to an H-back, depending on the players. He is interested in which blocker reaches the linebacker cleanly and whether the runner stays on that block long enough.

Trap is an answer he likes against an interior defender penetrating upfield. Jet sweep gives him a real outside handoff, with the same motion accompanying inside runs and play-action. He watches whether the defense widens, exchanges responsibilities or keeps chasing the first action.

He uses tight ends and motion to add a blocking surface or change the point of attack while retaining familiar blocking rules for each concept. His committee of backs gives him choices within that plan. He will return to a productive run until the defense changes the fit, then look at the complementary call he prepared for that response.

## 6. Defense

Stone delegates defensive calling to Crennel and stays involved in the plan. He wants the front and coverage working together, with enough disguise to make the quarterback confirm what he saw before the snap. His offensive background makes him especially interested in protection rules and the throws an opponent trusts under pressure.

### Pass Defense

His preference is to create pressure with four rushers and retain seven in coverage. He likes tackle-end games and changes in alignment that make the line pass off rushers. Against a mobile quarterback, he pays attention to the escape lane as well as the first pressure. Interior push matters to him because it takes away the quarterback's space to step up.

He likes man coverage with help placed where the opponent wants to throw. Cover 1 Robber appeals to him against digs and crossers: one safety stays deep while an underneath defender works the middle and the man defenders use their leverage to direct receivers toward that help. Against bunches and stacks, he wants the coverage exchange settled before the offense can use traffic to free a receiver.

Cover 3 gives him a zone changeup from a similar single-high picture. He also likes showing two safeties high and rotating late. He asks the underneath defenders which receivers they are carrying or passing off, particularly when an offense sends a seam route behind a crossing route.

His pressure packages attack a protection rule or a blocker. He will show six or seven near the line and send four, change which side supplies the rush, or drop a capable lineman into the expected quick-throw lane. When he adds a fifth rusher, he and Crennel identify the coverage behind it and the throw they are asking the defense to concede or contest.

### Run Defense

Stone wants the front controlling its assigned gaps and keeping the linebackers able to fit. He likes slants and coordinated exchanges that change the offense's blocking angles, with the linebacker fitting behind the movement. He judges penetration by what happens to the runner's path. A defender arriving in the backfield while the ball escapes through his gap gets a correction.

Against zone runs, he pays attention to the combination blocks and the backside cutback. He wants the line making it difficult for a blocker to climb cleanly, the linebackers staying connected to the runner's track and the backside pursuit closing without overrunning the play. The force defender keeps the outside boundary intact.

Against Power and Counter, he follows the puller through the fit. If the edge is assigned to spill the block, the support defender must be in position outside it. If the call is to box, the edge keeps outside leverage and turns the runner toward the inside help. He wants the safety, corner or nickel providing support to know which answer the front is playing.

Motion and quarterback runs receive their own preparation. A shifted tight end can change the gaps and the force assignment. Against zone read, he wants the handoff and quarterback responsibilities named, including any exchange between the end and linebacker. Those jobs remain explicit when the offense adds a moving blocker.

## 7. Opening Calls and Adjustments

Stone prepares an opening menu of roughly thirty calls. He uses familiar formations and concepts to find out how the opponent intends to defend him. Short yardage, a backed-up possession or another situational need takes him to that part of the sheet; unused opening calls remain available.

On the sideline, he stays relaxed and keeps the conversation moving. A frustrated quarterback gets a hearing. Stone asks what changed, which throws he is seeing clearly and which calls he trusts. He talks through the last series with the quarterback and staff, then settles on the answer for the next possession.

He separates a covered route from a late throw or a protection failure. If a defender is sitting in the dig window, he can work the shallow answer or another prepared concept. If the quarterback cannot reach the throw before the rush arrives, he changes the protection or timing. A rounded break goes back to the position coach.

His larger adjustments come from practiced football: quick game, screens, a different running emphasis or added protection. Route changes use shared tags and landmarks so the quarterback and receiver leave the conversation with the same call.

## 8. Personnel and System Flexibility

Stone starts a player with a job he can perform now. A receiving tight end can work from the slot or wing while his attached blocking develops. A powerful runner can contribute on early downs while learning protection. He changes the help and the assignment before giving up on the player.

He prefers a backfield committee with overlapping skills. He can feature the outside-zone runner, use another behind pullers and lean on his best protector in passing situations without making every substitution an announcement of the play.

An overmatched tackle can get a chip or protection help. An H-back can take a pulling job. A reserve entering the lineup gets calls he has practiced. Stone will remove a favored package when the available players cannot carry it, while retaining familiar terminology and useful parts of the offense.

A young quarterback may carry a smaller menu and add checks as he learns. Within the offense he knows, the quarterback has authority to change Stone's call at the line without fear of punishment for changing it. Stone reviews the read and the decision afterward, including successful plays that came from poor decisions.

## 9. Clock Management and Game Flow

With the ball, Stone keeps looking for points. A lead does not immediately turn him into a three-runs-and-punt caller. He wants the first downs that keep possession and the score that makes a comeback harder. A prepared pass remains part of his four-minute offense.

He discusses the acceptable outcome with the quarterback before those possessions: the covered throw, the sack risk, the opponent's timeouts and when to snap. As the situation changes, he can give greater weight to keeping the clock moving and forcing the defense to spend a timeout.

His defensive instinct with a fourth-quarter lead is more cautious. He prefers to keep completions in front, protect against the deep ball and tackle inbounds. With a narrow lead, that preference can leave him conceding short gains toward field-goal range. In a tie, he is more inclined to pressure the quarterback and look for a disruptive play.

Two-minute work has its own practiced procedure for the next call, inbounds completions and stopping the clock. Because he also calls the offense, he assigns an assistant to keep timeout, fourth-down and challenge information reaching him ahead of the decision. Stone makes the call.

## 10. Front-Office Relationships

Stone has no need to control personnel to feel that the team is his. In Jacksonville, Dave Caldwell has final authority over acquisitions, contracts and the cap. Stone brings his football judgment, argues his priorities and coaches the roster that results.

His requests take the form of detailed shopping lists. For an attached tight end, he specifies the edge blocks, combinations and routes the player will carry. He identifies what is needed immediately, what the staff can teach and where the player will need help. He is comfortable finding a role player through the draft, a low-cost signing or another team's cuts rather than demanding an expensive name.

His belief in development can make him more optimistic about a prospect than the personnel staff. He often sees a useful job and a way to teach the missing part. Caldwell can disagree about the time or roster place that work deserves. Stone accepts the decision without pretending the two evaluations were the same.

## 11. Player Development

Stone simplifies the teaching and explains why an assignment matters. The receiver learns how his route affects another receiver's space. The back learns whom his protection lets release. He wants that understanding available when the defense changes the picture.

He asks players which routes they trust, which blocks feel natural and where they hesitate. Their answers help shape the first assignment and the way he teaches it. He treats players as partners who can describe something useful about their own work.

Film work is specific. Freeze the picture before the snap. Identify the front or coverage clue. Have the player state the assignment. Run the play, locate the mistake and return to the point where the answer changed. Use correct and incorrect examples. Take the correction into a walkthrough, then a practice rep.

Quarterbacks connect their feet to the progression. Receivers work releases, depths and coverage adjustments. Backs work the run track, protection and receiving jobs. Linemen repeat footwork and exchanges. Defenders connect their alignment and key to the fit or coverage responsibility. Stone and the position coach judge what the player can execute after the explanation.

He makes room for reserves to receive repetitions and individual feedback. Increased responsibility follows work the staff can identify. A reduced role gets a direct explanation too. Dinner invitations and contact with a player's family continue through those conversations.

## 12. Public Accountability

Stone is measured, articulate and composed in public. He uses humor when it fits and speaks plainly when the team has played badly. His first response to criticism is to take responsibility for the call, the preparation or the situation he put the players in.

He protects individual players, including after an obvious mistake. He will blame his call when he knows the player missed the assignment. He takes the criticism himself and keeps the individual correction inside the building. Discipline is handled privately.

He is generous with specific credit. A good block, a reserve's preparation or an assistant's adjustment can get named even after a loss. After a poor defensive performance, he stays calm and discusses the problems and the adjustments ahead. He can acknowledge missed tackles or failed protection without turning the press conference into an accusation against one player.

In the meeting afterward, the actual mistake gets examined. The player receives the same precise feedback Stone would give after a win. His public defense does not depend on the player escaping that conversation.

## 13. Special Teams

Stone gives the kicking game meeting time, practice periods and weight in game-day roster decisions. A reserve's coverage work or protection can earn him a place among the active players.

The coordinator owns the technical plan. Stone remains involved in the choices that change possession and scoring: field goal, punt, fourth-down attempt or a prepared fake. He wants those decisions communicated before units begin coming on and off the field.

He reviews protection errors, coverage lanes, return decisions and penalties with the same attention he gives the offense. He enjoys a well-designed fake and wants to see the opponent's look and the players' execution in preparation before authorizing it.

## 14. Players and Their Families

Stone builds close relationships through meals, conversations and attention to players' families. Those relationships continue through injuries and poor stretches of football. A player can also keep parts of his life private without losing Stone's interest in his welfare.

He wants medical help reachable at any hour and access to multiple qualified psychologists. He encourages players to use that support. Clinicians control clearance, treatment and restrictions; practice demands stay within those restrictions and the applicable limits.

He wants a family liaison whom spouses and partners can approach about problems at home, including abuse by a player. Reports involving safety go to qualified help and the appropriate reporting process. He will not suppress a report to protect an important player or treat it as a disagreement he can settle over dinner.

A player he knows well can still lose snaps or a roster place. Stone explains the football decision directly. The relationship can remain strained afterward.
