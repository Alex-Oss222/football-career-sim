## Practice if Stone is out

Mike Tice runs practice. He has prior NFL head-coaching experience and already owns the offensive operating process and practice-script recommendations. Romeo Crennel is the second emergency lead if Tice is also unavailable.

Tice's authority is deliberately narrow: run the approved schedule, keep the periods on time, coordinate the three phases, and make ordinary situational adjustments. Crennel continues to run the defense and Westhoff continues to run special teams. Tice does not use Stone's absence to change the depth chart, install a new concept family, change a player's role, or make a disciplinary/personnel decision.

If Stone disappears in the middle of a practice, they finish the approved practice rather than turning it into a staff improvisation session. Medical staff retain all participation authority.

## Defensive relay backup

Daryl Smith stays the primary defensive communicator.

The succession is: Daryl Smith, then Paul Posluszny if medically cleared and in the relevant game package, then Julian Stanford.

Sio Moore learns enough of the communication structure to relay his packages, but I would not make him the automatic whole-defense backup yet.

During spring work, Crennel and Bush deliberately rotate the communication job without constantly changing the actual defensive roles. Stanford should receive full-call/echo/check reps even while Daryl is healthy.

For games, Jacksonville designates a primary and one backup radio helmet based on the active lineup. NFL rules permit two designated defensive radio helmets, but only one radio-equipped defender can be on the field at a time.

## Injured-player learning

Adopt it fully.

An injured player stays involved in whatever football learning the medical staff permits. Injury does not mean the player has to restart the playbook later, and missed physical reps are not treated as failed development.

Use four levels:

| Status | Football work |
|---|---|
| Full mental participation | Meetings, film, quizzes, protection/coverage ID, walkthrough observation |
| Reduced cognitive load | Short packets, short meetings, selected cutups only |
| Physical restriction only | Full mental work; no prohibited field action |
| Medical restriction from football work | No football obligation until clinicians allow it |

Return progression is recognize it, walk it, execute it non-contact, then prove the physical element when legal and cleared.

Especially with any head/neck issue, the doctors determine screen time, meetings, cognitive load and field access. Coaches do not decide that.

## Punt instruction

Westhoff owns the punt unit. Stone owns the decision to punt.

Every punt call gets an explicit purpose, in four families:

- **Field-position punt:** directional placement and coverage leverage.
- **Plus-territory punt:** placement and return suppression matter more than raw distance.
- **Backed-up punt:** operation security first (clean snap, protection, clean kick), then field position.
- **End-game punt:** clock, return risk, boundary, timeout situation and deliberate concessions are explicit.

Every punt is graded in three separate pieces:

- **Operation:** Cain to Anger, protection, timing.
- **Kick:** placement, distance, hang, direction.
- **Coverage:** release, lanes, leverage, tackle opportunity.

One bad return does not automatically become "bad punt." One great punt does not erase a protection mistake.

The spring progression has to respect the CBA. In Phase One, punters can kick but cannot work with a snapper/holder, while long snappers may snap into a net. Phase Two permits individual and "perfect play" work without offense-vs-defense competition; Phase Three permits non-contact unit work against an opposing look.

## Defensive intent

Crennel puts three things on every defensive package: TAKE, GIVE, ANSWER.

- **TAKE:** What are we trying to remove?
- **GIVE:** What are we consciously willing to concede?
- **ANSWER:** What changes when the offense motions, shifts, goes empty, bunches, changes the surface, or otherwise attacks the rule?

Example. TAKE: vertical access and the clean inside run. GIVE: underneath completion in front of us. ANSWER: motion changes leverage; safety echoes the adjustment and the front confirms the fit.

That is much more useful than players memorizing "Cover X, Front Y" without knowing why.

The position coach can then evaluate two different things: did the player understand the intended answer, and did he physically execute it? A corner losing a one-on-one the call knowingly exposed is different from the corner blowing the call.

## Consultation cadence

Keep it regular but short.

- **First active day of each week, 25 minutes:** Stone, Tice, Crennel, Westhoff. Install/load, health restrictions that have actually been communicated, open personnel questions, what needs evidence.
- **After every meaningful field session, 15 minutes:** the same four. Only: what held, what failed, what needs another look. No giant nightly staff summit.
- **Final workday of each week, 45 minutes:** coordinators plus relevant position coaches. Rep allocation, cross-training, injured-player learning, player feedback, special-teams problems, next week's teaching.
- **Every second week, 30-minute player council:** small rotating veteran/young-player group. What is clear, what is wasting reps, where communication is failing. It does not vote on the depth chart.
- **Training camp:** full staff review after every roughly three-practice block rather than after every practice.
- **Division rematches during the season:** veteran film input before the staff finalizes the adjustment plan.
