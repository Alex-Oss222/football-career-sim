## Player playbook access and evidence rule

Every participant may possess and study the **complete active 2013 Iteration I playbook**. The phase plan controls what the staff formally installs, practices, and evaluates together; it does not ration access to pages. A player may read ahead and ask about later material. He is evaluated on what has actually been assigned and taught for the phase.

No preset personnel percentage, snap share, rep quota, touch quota, or depth-chart probability determines the result of this phase. Reps and roles are adjusted from football evidence: assignment, communication, technique, physical execution, health, correction retention, matchup, special teams, and what the player demonstrates in practices and games. The plan creates opportunities to evaluate; it does not manufacture the answer.


This plan answers one question: **what does Jacksonville intend to teach, train, observe, and learn during rookie minicamp?** It does not pre-decide who succeeds, who earns a role, or who makes the roster.

The standard for this phase is **Good**, not perfect. A rookie is allowed to be new. He is not allowed to stay confused without communicating after the staff has taught and corrected the issue.

## 1. Pre-minicamp onboarding gate

Before a controlled rookie participates, confirm the onboarding steps that are legally and practically available have been completed:

1. Player control is verified. A draft-rights player, signed UDFA, tryout player, or other invitee must be correctly classified before the plan treats him as part of Jacksonville's roster.
2. The player receives the welcome package:
   - Stone welcome letter;
   - Prowl expectations pamphlet;
   - physical-readiness and medical-authority explanation;
   - support-resource map;
   - active 2013 Iteration I playbook;
   - position-room material;
   - rookie-minicamp roadmap.
3. Stone's individual follow-up call occurs approximately two days after package receipt when timing permits.
4. The player's position coach makes the room-specific follow-up call in the same general onboarding window.
5. Questions are routed to the right owner. Football teaching goes to coaches, logistics to Player Development, medical questions to medical staff, and physical-program questions to performance staff.
6. No role, depth-chart placement, or roster promise is created by the letter, the call, or a player's enthusiasm during onboarding.

If a late acquisition arrives too close to the event for the normal two-day sequence, do not fabricate a completed call. Give him the package, orient him lawfully, record the compressed timing, and complete the follow-up as soon as the calendar allows.

## 2. Minicamp objectives

Rookie minicamp is an orientation and baseline-evaluation environment. The staff wants to learn:

- Can the player absorb NFL terminology?
- Can he line up correctly?
- Can he communicate his assignment?
- Can he execute the first version of his technique?
- Does he retain a correction from one period to the next?
- Does he ask useful questions when he does not understand?
- Does his college skill translate to the job Jacksonville is asking him to perform?
- Can he function in a professional meeting, walkthrough, practice, treatment, recovery, and film-review routine?
- Does he have an obvious special-teams path that should receive more work later?
- What must the staff simplify, reteach, or individualize before the full veteran group is together?

The staff is **not** trying to answer every roster question in one rookie camp.

## 3. Teaching sequence

Every installed item follows the same cycle:

**Explain -> Show -> Walk -> Rep -> Correct -> Rep again -> Retain -> Add complexity.**

The staff should not skip directly from meeting-room explanation to high-speed evaluation and then blame the player for the missing bridge.

### Teaching block A — language and operation

Before expanding scheme volume, rookies learn:

- meeting-room terminology and note-taking expectations;
- huddle procedure;
- substitution and personnel identification;
- formation and strength language;
- cadence and snap-count discipline;
- where to stand between periods and how substitutions are communicated;
- basic pre-snap communication by position;
- what constitutes a question that must be asked immediately rather than saved;
- how correction will be delivered;
- how medical limitations are communicated without making the player defend his toughness.

### Teaching block B — position fundamentals

Each room identifies a small number of non-negotiable techniques. The purpose is not to rebuild a player's body of work in one weekend. The purpose is to establish Jacksonville's language for the fundamental.

Examples:

**Quarterbacks**
- huddle command and call repetition;
- cadence;
- exchange and ball security;
- drop/launch-point footwork appropriate to the installed concept;
- the beginning of the playbook's Pre-Snap Five;
- protection-point communication;
- progression discipline;
- end-of-rep communication with receivers and line.

**Running backs / fullbacks**
- stance and alignment;
- ball-security standard;
- core run-track landmarks;
- mesh/exchange mechanics;
- protection identification;
- scan/check technique;
- screen and checkdown landmarks;
- motion/shift procedure where installed.

**Wide receivers**
- Normal, Reduced, Tight, Wide, and Stack split language as assigned;
- stance/release;
- route landmark and depth;
- top-of-route body control;
- catch-and-finish standard;
- stalk/crack support rules where installed;
- formation movement without losing assignment.

**Tight ends / H-backs**
- attached, wing, flex, and move alignments;
- base run-block footwork;
- combination communication;
- release landmarks;
- protection role;
- route-job portability between Y/H/F-type responsibilities.

**Offensive line**
- stance, first step, leverage, hand placement, and finish position;
- center-point communication;
- base combination language;
- introductory use of MIKE, ACE, DEUCE, TREY, FAN, PINCH, LOCK, and ME/YOU only when those calls are part of the installed work;
- core run-family footwork;
- pass-set landmarks;
- twist/game communication in non-live teaching.

**Defensive line**
- alignment and stance;
- gap/responsibility language;
- strike, separation, leverage, and pursuit;
- edge/contain responsibility where assigned;
- rush-lane discipline;
- communication with adjacent linebackers/ends.

**Linebackers**
- formation/front recognition;
- run-fit responsibility;
- leverage and pursuit;
- coverage landmark;
- backfield key discipline;
- communication with the front and secondary;
- pressure responsibility only as installed.

**Defensive backs**
- alignment and leverage;
- coverage communication;
- transition/footwork standard;
- route recognition within the permitted teaching load;
- pursuit and tackling-position fundamentals without turning the phase into live tackling;
- force/support responsibility;
- special-teams crossover.

**Specialists**
- operation timing and communication;
- snap/hold/kick or punt mechanics;
- protection/coverage responsibilities appropriate to the role;
- emergency-operation awareness.

## 4. Offensive install: language before library

Rookie minicamp does not attempt the entire Iteration I offense.

The first offensive job is to make the language functional:

- formation call;
- strength;
- eligible labels;
- personnel;
- run direction;
- concept name;
- protection default;
- cadence;
- basic shift/motion rules;
- basic substitution communication.

The long-term 2013 core remains Power, Counter, Inside Zone, Outside Zone, Stick, Mesh, Drive, Y-Cross, Four Verticals, Boot Flood, and RB Slow Screen. Rookie minicamp uses only a **narrow, teachable subset** of those families at any one time.

A concept is eligible to expand only when the players responsible for it can:

1. line up correctly;
2. state the job;
3. communicate the adjustment;
4. execute the basic technique;
5. retain the most recent correction.

Do not add a second variant to hide the fact that the base version is not clean.

### Quarterback install standard

The rookie quarterback does not need mastery of every answer. He does need to begin operating like the offense's distributor:

- get the call to the huddle;
- break the huddle with enough time to operate;
- identify the protection point;
- understand the intended shell/box/leverage question for the installed call;
- know the first progression and immediate pressure answer;
- protect the football;
- communicate the error after the rep.

### Protection install standard

Start with the protection families attached to the installed concepts. SCAT, HALF, and other families are introduced only to the extent necessary for the actual minicamp menu. The line and quarterback must use one language. Do not let five linemen make five independent declarations.

## 5. Defensive install

The defensive staff begins with rules that allow rookies to play fast:

- alignment;
- assignment;
- leverage;
- gap/fit responsibility;
- coverage responsibility;
- communication;
- pursuit;
- tackling position and approach;
- rush-lane/contain responsibility.

The defense should not become exotic just because the whiteboard can handle it. Presentation may vary only after the underlying rule is understood.

Rookie defenders should leave minicamp knowing:

- where they align against the basic offensive structures shown to them;
- what their primary run responsibility is;
- what their primary coverage responsibility is;
- who communicates a change;
- where their help is supposed to be;
- what mistake the staff is trying to eliminate first.

One player may receive a narrower role than another. That is teaching triage, not a permanent ceiling.

## 6. Special-teams foundation

Every rookie who can plausibly contribute on special teams receives a real teaching path.

Teach:

- formation/alignment;
- stance;
- release or protection assignment;
- lane/leverage responsibility;
- block-avoidance fundamentals;
- return-team communication;
- ball-security responsibilities;
- substitution and emergency-backup procedure.

Special teams are not treated as filler after offense and defense finish. For many young players they are the first path to a game-day role, but no roster result is pre-awarded.

## 7. Physical and medical plan

Rookie minicamp establishes a baseline. It is not a punishment test.

Performance staff may observe or record, using period-appropriate tools and only within established rules:

- playing weight/body-composition information where appropriately collected;
- movement quality;
- acceleration/deceleration;
- position footwork;
- functional-strength indicators;
- repeat-effort response;
- recovery/treatment needs;
- technique degradation as reps accumulate.

Medical staff control diagnosis, restrictions, rehabilitation parameters, and clearance. Coaches receive the football status they need. A player's medical limitation is not converted into an effort grade.

There is no random running because a rookie made an assignment mistake.

## 8. Meeting and correction rhythm

Each major teaching block should have a correction loop:

1. install/preview;
2. walkthrough;
3. field period;
4. immediate position-room notes;
5. film review where available;
6. identify one or two corrections that matter most;
7. repeat the concept before adding volume;
8. verify the correction carried forward.

A position coach should be able to answer, for every rookie: **what are we asking him to fix next?**

General disappointment is not a coaching point.

## 9. Rookie evaluation standard

The phase uses **Good** as the target.

A player can be Good while still making mistakes if he:

- lines up correctly with normal rookie support;
- knows the assignment;
- communicates;
- plays with appropriate effort;
- executes the basic technique often enough to show the teaching is taking hold;
- accepts and retains correction;
- protects the football and teammates;
- handles the professional routine.

Separate the reason for a poor rep:

- assignment error;
- communication error;
- technique error;
- physical loss;
- processing delay;
- conditioning/recovery issue;
- medical limitation;
- coaching/teaching problem.

Do not collapse every bad rep into "player failed."

## 10. Family-inclusive team dinner

During the rookie-minicamp window, schedule a **Rookie Welcome Family Dinner** as a team-culture event.

- Invitation extends to all current Jacksonville players, coaches, relevant football/support staff, and each person's invited family or meaningful guest, even though the football portion of rookie minicamp is centered on rookies.
- Veteran participation in a rookie-window dinner is social and voluntary. It does not turn rookie minicamp into veteran football work.
- Family/guest attendance is always voluntary.
- The club makes the event practical for spouses/partners, children, parents, or another meaningful guest without requiring a player to explain his family structure.
- Stone gives only a short welcome. Coordinators and position coaches introduce themselves. There is no install meeting, no quiz, and no roster speech.
- Player Development makes resource contacts available without soliciting private stories.
- No attendance, guest choice, family circumstance, or dinner conversation enters a personnel grade.
- When the event actually occurs, record it in `rookie_minicamp/output.md`; do not rewrite this plan.

## 11. End-of-minicamp staff review

The staff should leave rookie minicamp with:

- a teaching-status note for every participant;
- the next correction for each player;
- position-room concepts that need reteaching;
- special-teams pathways worth expanding;
- medical/performance restrictions communicated through the proper staff;
- no invented final depth chart;
- no future-career hindsight;
- no conclusion based on whether the player was a draft pick, UDFA, or tryout invite rather than what he actually showed.

The review question is:

**What does this player need next so that Good can become Better when the full team work begins?**
