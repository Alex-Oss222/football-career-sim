# August 9 to 14, 2014

## Chicago Week: Revised Head Coach Planning Outline

### What changed after Tampa Bay

Game 1 did not give me enough first-unit football to simply put the veterans away for Chicago.

Cousins got six offensive snaps.

Both of his possessions went three-and-out.

Both sacks against his group came through the right-tackle slot.

The first offense never reached the bulk of the opening menu, HOLD, PRESS, the Empty shift or the shot package.

The starting defense played much more football, and its run fits held, but three completions of 57, 68 and 48 yards did most of Tampa Bay's damage. Until we see the film, I am not assigning those explosives to a player, technique or call.

The reserve offense gave us considerably more useful evidence:

- Henne's Linsley series produced a touchdown.
- Wilson ran the reserve core cleanly enough to produce an 80-yard touchdown drive, but later threw an interception at our 21.
- Shaw produced a touchdown drive.
- Bray received only one three-play possession.
- Anderson's zone work translated, but his pass protection was not tested.
- Hurns, Gabriel and Jackson handled real game work.
- the reserve offensive line operated, although Lucas also allowed a sack at right tackle.
- the defense produced seven sacks from seven players.
- Cain and Kreiter both had clean operations.
- Hawkins handled every named return successfully, including a 38-yard kickoff return.
- three Jacksonville turnovers remain part of the correction work.

The injuries also change the plan.

Lane Johnson is out following the trunk injury.

Cameron Brate is out long term and is removed completely from the Chicago football plan.

C.J. Anderson participates only when the medical staff clears him. I will not build the plan around a projected return date.

No competition was decided by Tampa Bay.

---

# What I want from Game 2

The identity of this game does **not** change.

This is still the rookies-and-backups game.

What changes is the beginning.

I want:

1. A short correction block for the first offense.
2. A short correction block for the first defense.
3. Then the rest of the night belongs to the rookies, backups and unresolved roster players.

The operating principle after those correction blocks remains:

**Play them. Cycle them. Change the problem. Put them back in.**

Game 1 showed me that the reserve core concepts can function.

Game 2 needs to tell me how many of these players can function repeatedly, under different conditions, after corrections and with changing people around them.

# ROTATE, HOLD and PRESS

## ROTATE

Still the dominant Game 2 operating state.

Use it to:

- cycle backs;
- cycle young receivers;
- cycle tight ends;
- expose players to entering cold;
- force the sideline to stay organized.

## HOLD

More important after Tampa.

The reserve offense showed it can run the core.

Now keep the same eleven and move them.

Example:

12 personnel:

1. Ace Power.
2. Trey Drive.
3. Wing Split Zone.
4. Shift toward Empty, Stick.
5. Return to Ace.

If a rookie only knows his formation and not his concept, this finds it.

The Tampa first group never reached its PRESS work.

The reserve PRESS package had already been cut from Game 1.

For Chicago:

- Cousins may run one short PRESS sequence if his correction block lasts long enough.
- Henne gets one.
- Wilson, Bray and Shaw do not automatically get one.

A developmental quarterback earns PRESS in the preparation first.

I am not testing communication by deliberately overwhelming it.

# Timeout philosophy

I keep the liberal Game 2 philosophy.

This is still the teaching and evaluation game.

I will use a timeout when it preserves a rare useful situation:

- reserve substitution collapses before the snap;
- quarterback and center are on different protections;
- young group cannot receive the call;
- a red-zone opportunity would otherwise be lost to an operation failure;
- two-minute communication requires one actual sideline cycle.

But I will not call timeout because a young tackle is about to lose a rep.

If he knows the assignment and gets beaten, I need to see it.

A timeout protects the evaluation from operational chaos.

It does not protect the player from football.
