## Ownership and GM review

*Wednesday, January 15, 2014. EverBank Field. Shad Khan, David Caldwell, Alex Stone.*

Khan began before Stone opened his review.

“You're coming back. Dave and I have discussed it. I don't want you spending this meeting wondering whether you need to defend your employment.”

“All right.”

“We won two games last year. This year we won ten and won at Kansas City in January. I'm pleased with the hire. I'm also interested in why we could do that and lose by roughly thirty points three different times.”

Caldwell had brought the regular-season totals, the two playoff reports and the personnel list. He removed the staples from the game reports, put the team comparisons beside the scoring summaries and passed Stone the second copy. He wrote 191 and 126 beneath the first eight games, then 165 and 220 beneath the last eight. He added the records above each column.

“Six and two, then four and four,” Caldwell said. “Sixty-five points better than the opposition in the first half. Fifty-five worse in the second. Every really bad loss came after the bye. That's more useful to me than saying we went from two wins to ten.”

“It's also more useful than calling us an eight-win team because we finished plus ten,” Stone said.

“I agree.”

Caldwell had counted the close games. Jacksonville was five and three in games decided by eight points or fewer. There were substantial wins over Kansas City, Indianapolis, Denver and Arizona. The second Tennessee game had reached 38-13 before the Titans reduced the margin. The ten wins did not require an extraordinary succession of last-play escapes.

“You earned the record,” he said. “I'm trying to decide how much of the football that produced it we can take into next September. Three afternoons account for ninety-one points of negative differential. We don't get to remove them. We also don't get to pretend the other thirteen games were all coin flips.”

The original agreement had asked for a functioning program, credible development and a quarterback direction. Caldwell said Stone had comfortably exceeded the first-year result the club had required. That did not turn every part of the original assignment into a completed job. The four-year contract gave them time to build; it did not make the recurring mistakes irrelevant until the fourth season.

Stone said the staff had stayed with its teaching process after the defeats. There had been no emergency change at quarterback, no wholesale replacement of the line, no new offense installed to demonstrate that something was being done. Jacksonville had beaten Arizona after the first Tennessee loss, Cleveland after Houston, and Tennessee after Buffalo.

“I liked that,” Caldwell said. “But we stayed with some things too long. Where do you put that?”

Stone named Houston.

The preparation had been explicit: establish the run, protect Cousins against the front, finish drives. Jones-Drew carried twelve times for eighty-six yards. Jacksonville finished with seventeen rushing attempts and forty-five dropbacks. Four possessions reached the Houston 24 or closer without producing a touchdown.

“I'm not defending the Houston sequence,” Stone said.

He defended the passing sequence after the long kickoff return. They were down 21-6, had started at the Houston 35 and had opportunities to score without abandoning the offense. A pass was not automatically wrong because the previous pass had failed. The final ratio also included the work after the deficit had become much larger.

Caldwell let him finish.

“I don't need you to tell me that throwing is allowed when we're behind. We were behind and getting useful runs. You said afterward that Houston hadn't stopped the run, we'd stopped calling it. Is that still your conclusion?”

“Yes.”

“Then that's the evaluation. The score explains some of the volume. It doesn't explain the whole afternoon.”

Stone had put a check into the following week's plan. After three consecutive called passes, he had to identify the football reason for another. It was not an automatic handoff. Down, distance, front and coverage still controlled the call.

“I can work with that,” Caldwell said. “What I want from Tice is whether it changes what you do when the game is getting away from you. If you ask yourself the question and approve your first answer every time, we've added words to the headset.”

Khan asked whether that was what had happened in the other two losses.

“Different failures,” Caldwell said. “The first Tennessee game was 17-11 before their long drive carried into the fourth quarter. We gave it away six times, missed two short kicks and then let it spread. Buffalo drove eighty-four yards and eighty yards for touchdowns on its first two possessions. We were down fourteen before the first turnover. We can't describe that as the defense being put in a bad position all day.”

“We didn't fit it,” Stone said. “They ran through us.”

“And Houston was both sides. I want the reports kept separate. If we call all three a preparation failure, we'll have three reports saying prepare better.”

They spent longer on the Houston rematch than on the first game. Jacksonville had carried the same basic offense into a short week, kept the run involved and converted eight of twelve third downs. Foster gained forty yards on seventeen carries. With Posluszny unavailable, Smith handled the communication and Allen started beside him. Smith's late forced fumble secured a one-point win.

“This is what I wanted to see,” Caldwell said. “We identified things we could change and played differently. I won't turn a one-point win into proof that every correction worked. The punt return still hurt us. But we made Houston defend a different game.”

Khan asked Stone what he believed he had established at quarterback.

“A starter. Kirk won the competition. He's earned the work going into next year.”

“For next year,” Caldwell said. “How far beyond that?”

“I don't have that answer yet.”

“That's fine. I need it stated that way. We bought the opportunity to find out. We didn't buy the answer.”

Caldwell had authorized the second-round pick for Cousins. He had also agreed to move Gabbert and keep Henne as insurance. He was not reviewing those decisions as though Stone had imposed them on an unwilling personnel department.

“The trade gave us sixteen starts of useful evaluation and a quarterback we could win with. I'm comfortable with what we spent. The pick is still gone. We don't have a second-round selection this spring, so every time we discuss another hole, that belongs in the discussion.”

Cousins had completed 63.3 percent of his passes for 3,981 yards, with twenty-three touchdowns, eighteen interceptions and thirty-eight sacks. Eight interceptions came in the London and first Tennessee games. Three more came against Buffalo. The concentration mattered. So did the four consecutive games without an interception at the end, beginning with the Tennessee rematch and continuing through both playoff games.

“You kept him in,” Caldwell said. “You made the correction smaller. He produced. That's a better argument for you than saying every turnover is on the coach.”

Stone's original review had assigned himself half the quarterback problem.

“I wouldn't divide it in half,” Caldwell said. “We need to know which ones were late, which ones were forced, which ones came from the receiver and which protections didn't give him an answer. Some are going to be his. Some are going to be ours. Taking responsibility doesn't finish the work.”

“Bates has the quarterback work. Tice and Yarno have the protection. I choose what goes into the game.”

“And your development plan says the correction has to survive a changed picture. That's the part I want back. Not that Kirk understood it on Tuesday. What happened when the defense showed him something else?”

There was another distinction Caldwell wanted kept. Cousins had protected the ball in Nashville. He had also thrown for 155 yards. Jacksonville converted one third down in nine tries and gained sixty-two yards over its eight possessions after the second-quarter touchdown.

“A clean interception column can't become the whole evaluation,” he said. “At Kansas City he protected it and hurt them. In Tennessee we protected it and couldn't stay on the field. Both games count.”

Stone said the answer would not be to strip every difficult throw out of the offense.

“All right. Because we need more from him than avoiding interceptions. The defense isn't going to hold everybody down while Josh keeps kicking field goals. Kirk showed us more than that at Kansas City. I want to understand why it disappeared the following week.”

Khan asked about the young players.

Stone started with Johnson and Thielen, then Poyer, Rambo and Kelce. Caldwell stopped him at Kelce.

“What did we get from the thirty-third pick?”

“A second tight end who can move. His attached work is still developing. Lewis was the more dependable complete player.”

“Eight catches.”

“Yes.”

“A hundred and five yards. No touchdowns. Seventeen targets. I approved taking him there because of the football projection you gave me. I'm not calling him a bad pick after one year. I'm asking why the second tight end in an offense that wants to use two tight ends was such a small part of the passing game.”

Stone went through the spring and camp evidence. Kelce's base and pad level in attached work had not been consistent. Changing the alignment could bring the correction back. Zernhelt had narrowed the job. Lewis had remained reliable as a blocker and receiver and finished with sixty-one catches for 720 yards.

“You don't take Lewis off the field to validate a draft choice,” Stone said.

“Of course you don't. But you can't put Kelce in the successful-development column simply because he dressed. We have to separate what he couldn't do from what we didn't ask him to do. If his blocking limited the package, show me that. If we kept using him to help protection, tell me what we gave up to get that help. If you didn't trust him as a receiver, I need to hear it.”

Kelce's fifty-three-yard catch at Kansas City was in the playoff report. Caldwell had left it there. It was evidence that the player could contribute in a meaningful game. It did not establish how frequently he could do it or why the regular-season role had remained so narrow.

“Thielen is a much stronger case,” Caldwell continued. “He came in without a draft position to protect. He learned the work, played special teams, earned a receiving role and kept it when Blackmon returned. Fifty-two catches. Six hundred sixteen yards. You moved him and got something useful. I can see the opportunity and the result.”

Johnson had started at right tackle. Keeping Monroe at left tackle had given the rookie a defined assignment instead of requiring the second pick to repair the entire line. Poyer had a real nickel role. Rambo had defensive and coverage work. Moore's limited role and Bray's place on the practice squad remained evaluations in progress.

“I'm encouraged by the class,” Caldwell said. “I'm still waiting on several of them. That belongs in the report.”

The center decision came up next. Meester had stabilized the early installation. Brewster replaced him during the season and kept the job after Meester became available.

Khan asked whether that had caused a problem.

“No problem that reached you,” Caldwell said. “That's useful. It isn't proof nobody disliked it.”

Stone said Brewster had earned the role and Meester had remained available as the reserve.

“That's the decision I hired you to make,” Caldwell said. “You didn't need to remove the veteran to make a point, and you didn't need to put him back because he'd been here longer. We should be able to explain the same decision to both of them.”

Blackmon required a longer discussion. The league suspension had cost four games. Stone's own inactive decision cost another two after reinstatement. When Blackmon dressed at Wembley, he was the third receiver, with Thielen retaining the expanded job.

“I backed the distinction between being eligible and having your old role handed back,” Caldwell said. “But those two extra inactive weeks are your decision. If we're evaluating your standards, they belong here. What football did we get for them?”

“He practiced. We could put him back with a defined job.”

“And he finished with thirty-two catches for 271 yards. I can't turn that into a success story about discipline. I can say we kept functioning while he was unavailable, and his return didn't displace somebody who'd earned the work. The rest needs more evidence.”

Khan asked whether anything else had happened that he needed to know about.

“Nothing additional in the record,” Caldwell said. “We shouldn't make more of it than that.”

Stone did not offer a personal account of Blackmon's recovery or describe what the receiver had said privately. They moved to the linebackers.

Posluszny's medical hold had removed a starter late in the season. Smith remained the communication lead. Allen played beside him. Against Houston, Allen recorded eight tackles and three for loss. At Kansas City, he intercepted Smith on the opening possession and returned the ball thirty-seven yards.

“That is what ready before you're needed looks like when it actually happens,” Caldwell said. “Bush and Romeo get credit. You get credit for organizing the staff and keeping the replacement's job manageable. Paul being unavailable isn't a mark against his preparation, and nobody has treated it as one.”

The medical hold remained in place. No return date was promised in the meeting.

Caldwell turned to the defensive totals. Fourth in yards allowed in this league. Third in opponent third-down percentage. Twenty-three sacks and fifteen takeaways, both near the bottom.

“Romeo gave us a defense that could get us off the field without needing a takeaway every time. That's a substantial part of the ten wins. We won London after four interceptions. The defense deserves to be in the first paragraph of that game.”

“It was,” Stone said.

“Keep it there in the season review too.”

Caldwell had no interest in treating the defense as a settled unit. Marks accounted for eight sacks. The edge production was thin. Injuries narrowed the secondary. Buffalo's opening drives could not be assigned to offensive turnovers, and the Tennessee rematch had exposed how much the pass defense could concede while correcting the run fits.

“I didn't get you Bennett,” Caldwell said. “We had a price and a structure, he wanted something else, and I stayed with our limit. That's part of the pass-rush story. It doesn't excuse every failure to finish a rush. It does mean I can't sit here acting as though you were given every player you asked for.”

Khan asked what the staff had cost.

Caldwell gave the scheduled assistant total: $7.225 million in 2013, excluding Stone and the other football and support departments. Crennel and Tice had three-year agreements. The other listed assistants had two-year agreements covering 2014.

“I wanted experienced people,” Stone said.

“You got them,” Khan said. “I'm happy to pay for people who make us better. I don't want keeping the same names presented as the result of the investment. What did the players learn? What did we stop doing badly? Where did the coaches make a difference?”

Caldwell pointed to the receiver development, the replacement linebackers and the changed Houston plan. He also pointed to the protection totals and the recurring difficulty finishing drives.

“There's enough here to keep working with this staff,” he said. “There isn't enough to say every room is working equally well. Tice has the weekday offense. You have the Sunday calls. I don't want a failure passed between those two descriptions until neither one owns it.”

Stone gave the staff position first. Alan Lowry had left on Sunday to become Atlanta's head coach, so the special teams coordinator job was open. Frank Bush had interviewed with Indianapolis the day before for its defensive coordinator job. The other assistants were under contract, and Stone was not recommending a change among them.

“I'm not asking you for one,” Khan said.

The kicking game still needed its own review. Scobee had made thirty-six of forty-one field goals. He had kept points coming when the offense stalled, although the two short misses in the first Tennessee game were part of that defeat. Caldwell would not treat the kicker's season as proof that the entire kicking game had been dependable. Keshawn Martin had produced damaging punt returns in both Houston games. Cleveland had a long kickoff return. Those plays belonged to the coverage evaluation even when Jacksonville won.

“We hired a special-teams coordinator and gave the young players jobs there,” he said. “Whoever replaces Alan starts from the same report we want from the offense and defense. Which units improved, who can stay in those roles, and what we're exposing when we dress the forty-six. Josh making the kick doesn't answer that.”

They discussed the support program briefly. The onboarding calls, individual teaching sheets and correction-and-retest process gave the club an organized way to teach. Family events and confidential support had defined boundaries. Their existence did not demonstrate that every player trusted the staff or that the resources had caused the wins. Neither Caldwell nor Khan had a report that could establish that.

“I like that the coaches are finding out what a player doesn't understand,” Caldwell said. “We've got examples of them changing the explanation and getting better work. Just make sure the reports include the players who still aren't getting it. I have to make decisions on those guys too.”

“You will.”

“You say that quickly.”

“You asked.”

Khan brought the discussion back to the club outside the football offices. Jacksonville had played meaningful games through December and won a road playoff game. People buying tickets did not need to be sold an abstract improvement in the organization. They had watched one.

“That matters to me,” he said. “It matters here, and it matters when we take this team to London. You gave us something people could watch and want to see again.”

The London preparation had reduced new installation after the main practice work and preserved time for walkthroughs, communication and recovery. Khan wanted that operational discipline maintained for Dallas the following season. He did not ask Stone to reproduce the four interceptions that the defense had survived at Wembley.

“What does next year need to look like?” Stone asked.

“Like we're building on this,” Khan said. “I know the schedule and the injuries and the roster will change. I won't tell you today that a particular number decides your job. But people will expect us to contend, and so will I. We can't spend next December explaining everything by what the team was before you arrived.”

Stone said the first priorities were keeping the lines together where possible, improving the corners and depth, and making Cousins more dependable under pressure. Written personnel recommendations would follow before the tag window.

“Bring me the football order,” Caldwell said. “What we need first, what can wait, and what changes if we can't sign the first choice. Don't make every room the first priority.”

“I will.”

“And separate continuity from keeping people. If the player can't do the job, we improve it. If the player can do it and we're replacing him, I want a reason that survives the cost.”

Khan had no contract proposal for Stone. Stone requested no raise, extension, additional authority or staff money. They continued under the agreement already in place: Caldwell controlled acquisitions, contracts, cap and scouting; Stone controlled his staff, football roles and game-day decisions. The quarterback consultation requirement remained a consultation requirement. The attempted concurrence right from the hiring negotiations had never become part of the contract.

Caldwell said he would put the personnel and development questions into the written follow-up. Stone asked him to include Kelce's blocking evaluation with the receiving questions, then returned to the page on which Caldwell had divided the season into two halves. Khan asked whether the February submission would include the players whose contracts still needed to be reconciled. Caldwell said it would, and began identifying which files he needed from football administration.

## Alex Stone: press exit interview

*Wednesday, January 15, 2014. Season-ending press availability. The questions are a fictional local press corps, not invented quotations attributed to real reporters.*

The communications staff connected the podium microphone, checked its level through the room speakers and set two recorders on the lectern. A camera operator asked for the microphone cable to be moved out of the aisle. The staffer moved it, counted the seats in the front row and told Stone they were ready.

“We finished the player meetings yesterday,” Stone said. “I met with Shad and Dave this morning. I'll be back under the contract I signed. We're reviewing the season and the roster. Dave will handle the personnel business. I'll answer what I can.”

**Reporter:** Did they tell you that you exceeded expectations?

**Stone:** Shad said he was pleased. We won ten games and a playoff game. He should be pleased with that. We also spent a good deal of the meeting on the games we played badly.

**Reporter:** Is this season a success?

**Stone:** Yes.

**Reporter:** You don't sound particularly enthusiastic about it.

**Stone:** We lost Saturday. That still counts too. But if you're asking whether the players accomplished something this year, they did. I'm not going to make them listen to me explain that eleven wins including the playoffs don't count because I can find something wrong with the last game.

**Reporter:** How much credit do you deserve for going from two wins to ten?

**Stone:** I hired the staff, organized the football and called the offense. Those are my responsibilities. Dave brought players in. The assistants taught them. The players played. I don't have a percentage for you.

**Reporter:** People will call it your turnaround.

**Stone:** They'll call it my team when we get beaten 38-6 too. I understand the job.

**Reporter:** Let's talk about that. Three losses by at least twenty-nine points. How does a team that wins a playoff game have that many afternoons where it looks completely overmatched?

**Stone:** We didn't fail the same way each time. At Tennessee we were still in the game in the third quarter and kept giving them the ball. Against Buffalo they drove the length of the field twice before we turned it over. Houston, we didn't get enough out of our possessions and they made plays against us in both phases. The common part is we let each game get well beyond us. The correction has to be more specific than that.

**Reporter:** Does that mean the team wasn't prepared?

**Stone:** It means the preparation didn't produce the football we needed. Some snaps were understood and physically lost. Some were decisions. Some were calls. If I tell you everybody was confused, that won't be true. If I tell you the plan was fine and the players just didn't execute, that won't be true either.

**Reporter:** Houston was the game where you said you stopped calling what was working. Why did it take a thirty-two-point loss to recognize that?

**Stone:** I should have stayed with it sooner. I saw throws I thought we could make and kept going back to them. By the end, the score was pushing us toward the pass, but we'd helped put ourselves in that position. I can't give you a better explanation than that.

**Reporter:** You have an offensive coordinator. Was Mike Tice telling you to run?

**Stone:** I'm not going to give you a reconstructed headset conversation. Mike helps put the plan together. I make the calls. The responsibility for getting away from it is mine.

**Reporter:** You say “mine” a lot. What actually changes?

**Stone:** After that game I put a check on consecutive pass calls. Not a rule that we have to run on the next snap. A check that I can explain why we're still throwing against that defense in that situation. We kept the run involved against Cleveland and in the Houston rematch. The results were different. We still have to make the right decision the next time we're behind.

**Reporter:** You also threw nine consecutive passes on the touchdown drive Saturday. Does that violate your correction?

**Stone:** No. Passing was producing on that drive. We scored. If I turn the check into a quota, I've made another bad rule. We needed an answer after that drive, when we stopped moving it.

**Reporter:** Eight possessions, sixty-two yards after the touchdown. Was Tennessee taking away your offense, or were you calling it too cautiously?

**Stone:** They defended us well. We didn't run it efficiently, we took sacks and we couldn't convert third down. One of nine. We were protecting the ball, but we weren't doing enough with it. You can call that cautious. I need to identify which calls gave us a chance and which ones didn't.

**Reporter:** Is Kirk Cousins your starting quarterback next season?

**Stone:** He's our starting quarterback. He earned the job and he's earned the work going forward.

**Reporter:** Is he a franchise quarterback?

**Stone:** I don't know what you're asking me to promise with that phrase. I know he can operate this offense and win games. I know there are things he has to improve. We've had one season together.

**Reporter:** Eighteen interceptions is a lot.

**Stone:** It is. Eight came in two games. That doesn't excuse them. It tells us something about how those games went wrong. He also finished the last four games without one, including both playoff games. We need to keep that part and produce more than we did Saturday.

**Reporter:** Did you consider benching him after the first Tennessee game?

**Stone:** I kept him as the starter. That was the decision. I believed we had corrections we could make with him. I'm not going to invent a conversation about benching him to make that decision sound harder.

**Reporter:** Do you owe Chad Henne a real chance to compete if he comes back?

**Stone:** Chad gets an honest evaluation of his work. Kirk is ahead of him because of what he's done. I'm not going to announce an even competition when it isn't even today. Chad's contract is a discussion with Dave and his representative.

**Reporter:** You paid a second-round pick for Cousins. Does that make you more reluctant to move on if he doesn't improve?

**Stone:** It shouldn't. The pick is spent. We evaluate the player we've got. Right now he's earned the position. That answer comes from the football, not from trying to win the trade again.

**Reporter:** You brought in a quarterback coach and an offensive coordinator with quarterback experience, drafted a tackle second overall and still gave up thirty-eight sacks. Why should people believe protection will improve?

**Stone:** Because we can identify work that improved and work that hasn't. Not because I can list the coaches. Some of those sacks were blocks we lost. Some involved the quarterback, the back, the route or the call. We need to make changes from those causes. Drafting Lane didn't make all eleven assignments work automatically.

**Reporter:** Are you satisfied with Lane Johnson?

**Stone:** He earned the right-tackle job and played through the season. He has technical work to do. I want him building on a full season of experience. I don't need to declare what his career is going to be today.

**Reporter:** You took Travis Kelce thirty-third. Eight catches. Did you misuse him?

**Stone:** That's a fair question. His role stayed limited. Marcedes played well, and Travis had attached work we needed to keep teaching. But the staff has to account for the opportunity we gave him. We can't answer you by saying he's young and leave it there.

**Reporter:** Fifty-three yards on one catch in Kansas City. Doesn't that suggest he should have had more chances?

**Stone:** It gives you a reason to ask. It gives us a rep to study. It doesn't tell us that every job was ready. We're reviewing the things he could have done more often as well as the things that limited him.

**Reporter:** Adam Thielen passed players who had much more invested in them. What did you see that other people missed?

**Stone:** I don't know what everybody else saw. He learned the assignments, ran the routes where they were supposed to be and helped us on special teams. He kept earning work. Moving him gave us useful matchups. Darryl deserves credit for the teaching, and Adam deserves credit for doing it.

**Reporter:** Was it difficult keeping him ahead of Justin Blackmon?

**Stone:** Adam had earned the job. Justin's return didn't erase that.

**Reporter:** The league suspension was four games. You kept Blackmon inactive for two more. Do you believe that worked?

**Stone:** I believed he needed that period practicing before we put him back in a game. That was my decision. I'm not going to tell you his receiving total proves a disciplinary decision worked. When he dressed, he had a football role and we evaluated it.

**Reporter:** Did you cost the team two games of one of its more talented players unnecessarily?

**Stone:** I chose to make him inactive. You're entitled to question the cost. I believed it was the right decision then. I don't have anything from this review that changes it.

**Reporter:** Can the organization rely on him?

**Stone:** We evaluate what he does when he's available and manage his status through the proper process. I'm not going to make a guarantee about anybody's future conduct. I'm also not going to discuss private treatment or conversations here.

**Reporter:** Your program puts a lot into player support. Is that effective if you still have these problems?

**Stone:** The resources are there for players to use. They aren't a guarantee that nobody has a problem. We can tell you whether the football operation is functioning. We aren't going to prove the value of confidential help by telling you who asked for it.

**Reporter:** The defense was fourth in yards allowed. Is Romeo Crennel getting enough credit?

**Stone:** He should get a lot. He calls it. He and the defensive staff prepared it. We won games in which our offense made their job difficult, and they kept playing. London is one example. I'm the head coach, but I wasn't calling Romeo's defense from behind him.

**Reporter:** Twenty-three sacks and fifteen takeaways. Can that defense take the next step without a better pass rush?

**Stone:** We need more disruption. We also need to keep the things that made it hard to sustain drives against us. I don't want to give an offense an easy completion because we decided we had to look more aggressive. Personnel is part of that discussion with Dave. So is improving the players here.

**Reporter:** Paul Posluszny was out for the stretch run. How much did that affect you?

**Stone:** We lost a good player. Russell went in, Daryl kept the communication, and the staff adjusted the jobs. Russell helped us win important games. Buffalo still ran for 237. I won't use Paul's absence to explain every bad fit, and I won't pretend losing him didn't matter.

**Reporter:** Will he be ready for the offseason?

**Stone:** That's a medical question. We plan from the availability the medical staff gives us. I can't give you clearance from here.

**Reporter:** Are you keeping all your assistants?

**Stone:** Alan Lowry is Atlanta's head coach, and he earned that job. Special teams coordinator is open. The rest are under contract, and we're reviewing the work in every room.

**Reporter:** Do you expect other teams to ask to interview them?

**Stone:** Indianapolis asked to talk to Frank Bush about its coordinator job, and we said yes. That's his conversation to discuss, not mine. I don't have anything else to announce.

**Reporter:** At Indianapolis, you had the division in reach and lost by a point. Then there was the late punt in Nashville. Are you satisfied with your end-of-game operation?

**Stone:** No. After Indianapolis we put an explicit end-game check into the plan: score, down, distance, clock, timeouts, field position and the consequences of a punt. Lowry was part of that conversation. The operation belongs to me.

**Reporter:** But you still punted in Tennessee, down seven, with 2:22 left. Why?

**Stone:** The result was that they used almost all the time and we got it back at our eight with seven seconds. I accepted responsibility after the game. I'm not going to give you a neat explanation today that I can't substantiate.

**Reporter:** So you can't defend it?

**Stone:** I can't defend what we got from that finish. I also won't pretend that changing one decision accounts for eight possessions without a touchdown. The offense left us in that position. Both need to be reviewed.

**Reporter:** Does holding the play sheet make it harder to run the whole game?

**Stone:** It adds work. That's why the responsibilities around me have to be clear. Tice runs the weekday offensive process, Romeo calls the defense, and until we hire a special teams coordinator, the kicking game is mine too. I'm responsible for how the whole team plays. I need to know when to get involved and when to let a coach finish his work.

**Reporter:** Did ownership ask you to give it up?

**Stone:** No change in my responsibilities came out of the meeting.

**Reporter:** Did you ask for an extension?

**Stone:** No.

**Reporter:** A raise?

**Stone:** No. Same contract.

**Reporter:** Is it playoffs or failure next season?

**Stone:** We're preparing to contend. I'm not going to negotiate a public firing condition with you. Shad and Dave will evaluate the team and my work.

**Reporter:** That sounds less ambitious than what your players are going to say.

**Stone:** The players should want to win the division and win in the playoffs. So should I. Making a guarantee in January doesn't prepare us for either one.

**Reporter:** Maurice Jones-Drew had 1,334 rushing yards and twelve total touchdowns. Do you want him back?

**Stone:** I value what Maurice did. He ran, caught the ball and helped the offense function. He knows that. The contract discussion has to include Dave. I won't negotiate his terms through this microphone.

**Reporter:** Eugene Monroe?

**Stone:** We put a rookie on the right and kept Eugene on the left for a reason. His contract is one of the important decisions ahead of us.

**Reporter:** What's the first thing you change on offense?

**Stone:** We're still completing the review. Protection and ball security remain work. Finishing drives has to be in that discussion too. We kicked five field goals at Indianapolis. We won at Kansas City by finishing drives. We need the offense to produce that kind of football more reliably.

**Reporter:** Do you need more players or better coaching?

**Stone:** We'll improve the roster where we can. We have work to do with the roster we have. Dave needs a specific request from me. The players need a specific correction. Neither one is helped by that question having one answer.

The communications director said there was time for two more. A reporter near the aisle asked whether Stone would make the assistants available before they left for offseason scouting work. Stone said the staff would arrange it. Another reporter asked to return to the Blackmon decision, and the communications director told the camera crew they would need another minute.

## February 2: what Caldwell should make of the actual offseason proposal

Stone's written proposal is more aggressive than the direction he gave in the January meeting. That matters to the assessment of him. The coach who asks to preserve the lines and build on an established offense subsequently proposes replacing or moving several of its working parts. Caldwell should judge the actual submission, including its dependencies, rather than treating the January language as the plan.

The strongest portions are the retention priorities and the refusal to spend another pick casually. Monroe protects the left side while Johnson develops on the right. Marks supplies a disproportionate share of the defensive pressure. A short Jones-Drew agreement acknowledges both the production and the cost of relying on an older back. Keeping an experienced reserve behind Cousins is sensible insurance. The proposal preserves the teaching method, gives returning players retention checks, and recognizes that a returning head coach does not receive the same extra offseason opportunity as a new one.

Those are coherent recommendations. They show that Stone understands useful continuity and can distinguish a player worth keeping from a player whose past service creates an emotional argument to keep him. The written walk-away points and fallback orders also give Caldwell something more useful than a list of names the coach likes.

The proposal is weaker when it converts those names into a nearly assembled future roster. Verner and Talib are targets, not Jacksonville corners. Tate is a target, not a signed receiver. The depth explanations and phase plans occasionally speak as though those acquisitions have already happened. That is consequential because the proposed departures draw their justification from the replacements. If a target remains with his current club, dislikes the terms or signs elsewhere, Jacksonville needs a functioning roster while the next negotiation proceeds.

Caldwell should make Stone defend the receiver spending first. The existing group contains Shorts, who caught 104 passes for 1,158 yards, and Thielen, whose role expanded on actual performance. Lewis caught another sixty-one passes. Kelce remains an expensive, underused developmental investment. This was not an offense without anyone to throw to. Its problems included protection, forced throws, finishing drives and sustaining offense against a familiar playoff opponent.

Tate could improve the group. Sanders could replace production if Shorts leaves. Neither proposition demonstrates that Jacksonville should buy both, entertain another slot-receiver pursuit and then spend much of its acquisition effort on reassembling receiving capacity it already possessed. Heavy personnel, protection help and additional receivers also compete for playing opportunities. Stone owes Caldwell a role description that explains who leaves the field, which problem improves and how Cousins benefits. A new receiver's production elsewhere is insufficient.

The proposed Seattle package makes that question unavoidable. Stone wants the thirty-seventh pick for Shorts and Blackmon, after signing Tate and a replacement receiver. He has sensibly made the trade conditional on replacing the players first, but that condition does not eliminate the risk. If Seattle declines, the new receiver obligations remain alongside the incumbents. The fallback preserves talent; it can also preserve more expense and overlapping roles than the original offseason needed.

Seattle's low receiver production is a reason to investigate a match. It does not establish Seattle's willingness to surrender that particular pick. The receiving total could reflect quarterback play, protection, offensive choices, receivers, or several of those factors. Shorts also has a limited remaining contract horizon. Blackmon brings a documented availability question and an unresolved guarantee issue in the branch. Jacksonville cannot value both men at their most attractive football projection and assume Seattle will price the risks the same way.

The package is therefore a negotiation worth evaluating, not an obvious recovery of the Cousins pick. Getting back into the second round has no independent value if the cost of arranging it exceeds the football gained. Caldwell should ask Stone what Jacksonville would do with the receivers if no pick were missing. That answer would help establish whether the proposal improves the team or repairs the appearance of its draft inventory.

The corner plan deserves a different discussion. Ball's injury exposed the depth, and the defense needs to withstand good receivers without distorting its run fits. Acquiring a high-quality outside corner has a direct football case. Pursuing two may also have one. The unresolved part is the order of spending relative to an edge group that produced little pressure and an offensive line the coach still wants to rebuild. Caldwell should require a workable plan after one major target signs, and another if none signs. Listing fallback names is the start of that work. It does not guarantee that the fallback player will still be available when Jacksonville reaches him.

The plan contains useful safeguards around several trades. Allen does not leave before Posluszny's clearance and the draft. Rackley or Brewster does not leave before two linemen are drafted. Babin stays if the veteran-rusher alternatives disappear. Those conditions show more discipline than a simple clearance list.

They are also incomplete football tests. Medical clearance for Posluszny does not by itself establish that the linebacker group can absorb another injury. Allen has already supplied valuable replacement play in meaningful games. A late future pick has to compensate for losing that capacity. Similarly, drafting two linemen establishes ownership of two prospects. It does not establish that either can identify a pressure, hold up physically or replace a useful veteran by September. Stone's own development rules require demonstrated execution before responsibility expands. Caldwell should apply that same standard to the proposed exits.

The plan's language about continuity is especially exposed at guard and center. Nwaneri is scheduled to move before a contractual deadline, while another incumbent interior lineman may move after the draft. Some change may be justified by cost and performance. But Stone cannot ask for better protection communication while treating established communication as free to replace. The coaching department should explain the transition cost, identify the player who carries the calls, and show what happens if a rookie needs the limited role that Kelce needed in 2013.

The futures recommendations are the least expensive decisions and some of the least persuasive evaluations. The submission cites a lack of branch evidence when recommending departures, but several players it wants back also lack meaningful documented evidence. A missing record cannot establish that one player failed while becoming an invitation to imagine another player's potential. Age matters to projection. Age alone does not settle a fringe roster evaluation. Caldwell should ask for practice assessments, special-teams utility, medical availability and the cost of the alternative before accepting the distinction.

The contract detail requires restraint. Stone supplies useful offers, ceilings and preferred structures. He also states an available-room range while listing unresolved carryover, inherited guarantees, salaries and bonus dates that affect that room. Those figures can support scenario planning. They cannot support an assertion that the full plan is already funded. The price a player accepts, the clauses protecting that price and the order in which obligations become available all still belong to Caldwell's staff to establish.

There are two errors Caldwell should correct without turning the meeting into a lecture. The memo credits Verner with two interceptions against Jacksonville in Week 10; the branch receipt records one. The acquisition sequence also says to sign players during March 8-11 while another part correctly states that signing cannot begin before the league-year opening on March 11. The intended football pursuit is clear. The execution language needs to be consistent before anyone acts on it.

Neither error invalidates the board. They do weaken any attempt to present its level of detail as proof that the work is finished. Stone's clearest strength is organizing a football idea into assignments, teaching and contingencies. His recurring risk is treating that organization as sufficient evidence that the idea will work. Kelce's receiving role, the Houston plan and the proposed roster sequence each require a further question: what actually transfers when the conditions change?

Caldwell should retain Stone's football priorities while requiring a narrower first sequence: secure the essential incumbent decisions, reconcile the spending capacity, identify the most valuable external improvement and test the replacement players before disposing of useful depth. That is a recommendation for handling this submission, not a set of completed moves or a claim that Caldwell has already accepted it. The proposed Seattle exchange remains open, as do the contract terms and the individual evaluations the memo has not supplied.

## Research record and limits

The manuscript's overall judgment is favorable because the branch evidence warrants it. A first-year head coach who turns a 2-14 club into a 10-6 team with a decisive road playoff victory has earned substantial organizational confidence. Retention is not plausibly a narrow escape made possible by a guaranteed contract. The uncertainty concerns how strong the underlying team is, whether specific corrections last, and whether Stone can make a better acquisition case than the broad assertion that the program worked.

The dialogue and meeting details are dramatic reconstruction. They communicate an evidence-based interpretation of the owner and GM's incentives; they do not claim access to their real private views. No current player, assistant or reporter is assigned an unrecorded grievance, interview request or private misconduct; the staff departures and interview requests in the corrected passages are the ones ledger Entry 75 records. The draft makes no new binding promises on Stone's behalf.

### Material limitations preserved

1. **The Indianapolis finish is not a valid clock-management case study.** The Week 17 report places a punt at three seconds, then supplies a return and two offensive completions. It cannot support that elapsed-time sequence. The final score remains closed branch canon. The review uses the documented five field goals, one touchdown, failed finishing and later procedural response, but does not invent a defensible real-time account of the ending.
2. **The Divisional punt was not a recorded user decision.** The Tennessee report explicitly identifies it as an engine draw, and the season ledger states that games ran with autonomous game management and no in-game decision entered. The press can question the publicly recorded punt and Stone's already recorded acceptance of responsibility. The draft supplies no invented timeout inventory, hidden rationale or claim that the user deliberately selected it.
3. **Play labels and some individual attribution have known limits.** The ledger records post-resolution call labeling and statistical attribution defects. The manuscript does not infer a receiver's actual coverage read, a lineman's culpability for a sack, or a specific coaching failure from a label alone. The larger documented results and the coach's explicit planned corrections carry the evaluation.
4. **The development plan misnames the quarterback coach.** It says “Greg Bates”; the executed staff register and hiring record identify Jeremy Bates. The manuscript follows those controlling staff records.
5. **January 15 is an information boundary.** The later award draw, Buffalo's eventual championship, later medical clearances and the February 2 personnel memo are not facts known in the January scenes. In particular, Buffalo's later title does not retroactively excuse the Week 15 loss.
6. **A functioning process is not proof of an unrecorded outcome.** Staff continuity establishes continuity. A written support policy establishes a policy. A rookie dressing establishes availability and role, not successful development by itself. No private player survey, coach disagreement or commercial revenue gain was invented to fill those gaps.
