# INTERVIEW-ONLY POSITION

These are positions Alex Stone is prepared to discuss in January 2013 interviews.

They are not permanent post-hire scheme canon.

They do not establish that any assistant has agreed to join Stone.

They do not establish what a team will look like before Stone and the general manager evaluate the roster.

## Jacksonville Jaguars

### The starting point: establish an identity

Year one starts with establishing an identity.

Not an offensive system. Not a defensive front. Not a playbook Stone already has sitting in a binder waiting to be imposed on Jacksonville.

An identity.

The first question is: what are the Jacksonville Jaguars going to believe they are?

That has to mean something to the players and coaches. It cannot just be a slogan painted in the building. It has to show up in how we practice, how we prepare, how we play situational football, how we evaluate players, and eventually how we acquire players.

But Stone cannot honestly tell David Caldwell what the football identity will look like on the field before he has evaluated the team.

If Jacksonville has an outstanding tight end room, that should matter.

If the strength of the roster becomes the running game, that should matter.

If the quarterback's best football comes from movement, quick decisions or a particular passing structure, that should matter.

If the offensive line dictates that the offense has to operate differently, that matters too.

Same thing defensively.

Stone is not walking into Jacksonville in January and announcing that the Jaguars are now a 3-4 team, a 4-3 team, a West Coast team, a power-running team or anything else before the staff has evaluated who can actually play.

The philosophy comes first.

Evaluate honestly.

Teach well.

Make players do things they can actually execute.

Become dependable in situations that decide games.

Build a team whose offense, defense and special teams complement one another.

Then the identity becomes more specific as the roster tells us what it can become.

Year one establishes it.

Year two strengthens it.

Year three adds to it.

Year four should look like a mature football program, but it should not necessarily look exactly like whatever we installed in September of 2013.

Good teams evolve.

### Offensive approach

Stone keeps the offensive play-calling responsibility.

That does not mean Stone walks in carrying "the Alex Stone offense."

The offense starts with an evaluation of the quarterback room, offensive line, backs, tight ends and receivers.

There will be a core language and a core set of concepts because players need continuity. Stone is not changing the offense every Sunday.

But the distribution changes according to personnel.

If the running game is the best thing Jacksonville does, run it.

If the quarterback handles empty formations extremely well, use them.

If the protection cannot hold up for long-developing passes, stop calling football that requires it.

If the tight ends create matchup problems, use them.

That sounds obvious, but teams get themselves in trouble when a coach becomes more loyal to his scheme than to the players standing in front of him.

The goal is eventually to have enough answers that Jacksonville can attack different defenses without becoming a completely different offense every week.

Stone does not promise Caldwell that Blaine Gabbert starts.

He also does not walk into the interview saying Gabbert cannot play.

Evaluate him.

Evaluate every quarterback in the building.

Evaluate the draft.

Evaluate every other legitimate avenue available.

Then make the football decision.

The No. 2 pick does not automatically have to become a quarterback simply because Jacksonville needs better quarterback play.

### Defensive approach

Stone hires a defensive coordinator to run the defense.

Stone has enough defensive background to challenge the plan and understand what is being taught, but he is not going to stand behind the defensive coordinator and secretly coordinate that side of the ball.

The first defensive question is personnel.

Who can rush?

Who can cover?

Who can tackle?

Which defensive linemen are actually worth building around?

What can the linebackers handle?

What does the secondary allow us to call?

Then the coordinator builds around the answers.

Stone wants a defense that can communicate, tackle, prevent cheap explosives and get off the field on third down.

How it accomplishes those things is not being permanently decided in this interview.

### Jacksonville staff board

The first offensive conversation would be Aaron Kromer.

Not because Stone is promising him the job and not because Jacksonville has already decided to become a particular running team.

Kromer has worked directly inside the New Orleans offensive structure, understands Stone's game-day responsibilities, has substantial offensive-line and run-game responsibility, and could carry a meaningful part of the weekday offensive operation while Stone calls the game.

That matters because Stone cannot become permanent head coach and continue functioning as though his only responsibility is the offensive call sheet.

A second offensive path can be explored if Kromer is unavailable or does not want the role.

Defensively, Steve Spagnuolo is a legitimate conversation because Stone has just spent a full season supervising him.

That is not an automatic continuation.

Stone would have to decide whether he believes the football fit is right for Jacksonville, Spagnuolo would have to want the job, and New Orleans contractual circumstances would have to permit it.

Romeo Crennel is another legitimate defensive conversation. There is previous New England professional overlap, he has extensive defensive experience, and he is on the market.

Again, conversation, not commitment.

### Caldwell relationship

Stone should not walk into David Caldwell's first head-coaching hire and demand to become the general manager.

Caldwell runs scouting.

Caldwell runs contracts.

Caldwell runs the salary cap.

Caldwell administers the draft process.

Stone's responsibility is telling Caldwell exactly what the coaching staff needs from a player, how a player projects into the program, and whether the coaching staff believes it can develop him.

The major decisions have to involve both of them.

Quarterback especially.

Neither man should be able to force the organization into a major quarterback commitment that the other fundamentally does not believe in.

If they disagree, they keep working the evaluation.

Put the scouting report on the table.

Put the coaching projection on the table.

Put the cost on the table.

Then make the decision.

### Year-one standard

Stone is not promising David Caldwell a playoff appearance in 2013.

Jacksonville just went 2-14.

The first year has to produce evidence that an actual football program is being built.

Players know what is expected.

The staff teaches the same standards consistently.

Young players improve.

The team becomes harder to beat because it stops giving games away through bad preparation, poor communication and repeated mistakes.

The roster begins revealing which players should be part of the longer build.

Quarterback gets evaluated honestly.

And by the end of the season, Jacksonville should be able to explain what kind of football team it is becoming.

That is the first year.

Not pretending the fourth year can be installed in the first month.

## Arizona Cardinals

Arizona is different.

Stone should not sell Michael Bidwill and Steve Keim a Jacksonville-style rebuild because that is not necessarily what the Cardinals need.

The first job is to make the football operation function again.

### Arizona identity

The identity starts with becoming dependable.

Arizona cannot properly evaluate its quarterback situation if the offensive operation itself is dysfunctional.

That does not mean Stone walks into the room saying, "We're an offensive-line team now."

It means protection has to become an organizational priority.

That may involve personnel.

It may involve coaching.

It may involve scheme.

It will probably involve all three.

But Stone should not decide the solution before evaluating the problem.

Kevin Kolb does not get promised the job.

He also does not get eliminated before Stone coaches him.

The seventh pick does not automatically become a quarterback.

The evaluation determines that.

### Offense

Stone builds the offense around whichever quarterback actually wins the position and around the five-man protection unit the club can construct.

The offense has to give the quarterback answers.

Sometimes that means getting the ball out quickly.

Sometimes it means helping protection.

Sometimes it means running the football enough that the defense cannot spend the afternoon attacking the quarterback.

Sometimes it means creating deeper opportunities for the receivers.

Those percentages come later.

Stone should not pretend to know them in January.

### Defense

The first defensive conversation is Ray Horton.

That is the respectful and football-sensible thing to do.

Horton has already run the defense.

The players know his system.

The organization knows his work.

Stone should sit down with him and determine whether Horton actually wants to remain under a head coach who just got the job Horton himself interviewed for.

Do not assume that answer is yes.

If Horton wants to remain and Stone believes their working relationship can function, there is no reason to blow up a productive defense for cosmetic reasons.

If Horton wants to leave, let him leave professionally and move to the next candidate.

Romeo Crennel belongs on that contingency list.

### Offensive staff

Kromer is again a sensible first conversation because offensive-line improvement is central to what Stone is telling Keim.

Pat Shurmur is also worth a conversation.

Shurmur gives Stone another experienced offensive coach who has worked extensively with quarterbacks and can carry substantial weekday responsibility.

Stone remains the game-day caller.

### Keim relationship

Stone and Keim should have clearly separated responsibilities.

Keim manages the personnel department.

Stone defines what his staff needs and how it intends to use players.

Neither one gets to substitute authority for an argument.

If Stone tells Keim a lineman cannot execute what the offense requires, Keim has to take that seriously.

If Keim tells Stone the personnel department cannot acquire the exact player Stone wants at a rational cost, Stone has to take that seriously too.

That is collaboration.

It does not mean they agree about everything.

### Year-one standard

Figure out the quarterback position.

Improve the conditions around that quarterback.

Get the offensive line moving in the right direction.

Preserve defensive strengths instead of destroying them simply because there is a new head coach.

Establish a working relationship between Stone and Keim that can survive disagreement.

That is enough of an agenda without inventing a complete Arizona identity before the roster has been evaluated.

## Philadelphia Eagles

Philadelphia is the job where Stone can talk more openly about offensive flexibility because the roster already presents several very different possibilities.

That still does not mean he decides the offense before taking the job.

### Philadelphia identity

The Eagles should become difficult to prepare for without becoming difficult for their own players to understand.

That is the philosophy.

There are useful offensive players already in the building.

Stone does not need to demolish the offense simply so the new staff can say it built something.

The question is how those pieces fit together and, above all, who should play quarterback.

Michael Vick gets evaluated.

Nick Foles gets evaluated.

Neither one gets the job in the interview.

Neither one gets shipped out in the interview.

The fourth pick does not become a quarterback automatically.

### Quarterback flexibility

Stone should resist one mistake here: presenting two entirely separate offenses.

If Vick wins the job, certain calls and concepts are naturally going to emphasize things Vick does well.

If Foles wins it, the distribution changes.

But the entire offensive staff cannot carry two languages and two unrelated systems through training camp.

Build one offense.

Then tailor the call sheet to the quarterback.

That is very different from forcing both quarterbacks to play exactly the same way.

### Staff

Pat Shurmur is a very credible offensive coordinator conversation for Philadelphia.

He has significant prior experience in the organization, has been a quarterback coach, offensive coordinator and head coach, and is available.

Stone would still call the offense.

Shurmur's job would be substantial: offensive meetings, weekly planning, quarterback preparation, installation coordination, and making sure Stone's head-coaching responsibilities do not turn the offense into a one-man operation.

Steve Spagnuolo is a legitimate defensive call.

He has both direct recent working experience with Stone and previous experience in Philadelphia.

Again, that is a call Stone wants to make, not a commitment already in his pocket.

### Roseman relationship

Howie Roseman controls contracts, cap mechanics, scouting administration and transactions.

Stone has a major voice in football fit.

The phrase "joint decision" cannot mean everybody gets to take credit for good moves and nobody is responsible for bad ones.

Before major personnel decisions, Roseman's department should produce the personnel evaluation.

Stone's staff produces the coaching projection.

Then they argue the football case.

Stone does not need final control of every transaction.

He does need to know that when the organization acquires a major player, especially a quarterback, the coaching staff actually believes in what it is being asked to build.

### Year-one standard

Philadelphia can move faster than Jacksonville.

That does not mean Stone promises a record.

The first season should answer major questions quickly.

Who is the quarterback?

Which existing offensive players fit the next version of the team?

Which parts of the roster need repair and which parts simply need better organization?

Can the offense develop a core language that survives personnel changes?

And can Stone build an operation that does not depend on him personally touching every detail?

That last question matters because Lurie is not just hiring an offensive coordinator.

He is hiring the head coach of the Philadelphia Eagles.

## Chicago Bears

Chicago gets the shortest philosophical pitch because Stone should recognize what is sitting in front of him.

This is not a reclamation project.

The Bears won ten games.

Stone is not walking into the interview pretending everything is broken because that would be ridiculous.

### Chicago identity

Preserve what works.

Fix what does not.

The defense has already established credibility.

Do not tear it apart because the head coach happens to come from offense.

The primary football problem Stone is being hired to address is the offensive operation.

That is where his attention goes first.

### Offense

Jay Cutler gets the 2013 season as an evaluation.

No pre-interview declaration that Cutler is the long-term quarterback.

No pre-interview declaration that he needs to be replaced.

Coach him.

Build the best offense the current roster can support.

See what happens.

Chicago already has obvious established offensive players. Stone would be lying if he acted like Matt Forte and Brandon Marshall did not matter to the evaluation.

They matter.

But even knowing that does not tell Stone what the complete offense should be.

The offensive line has to be evaluated and improved.

Protection needs to become dependable enough that Stone can find out what Cutler actually looks like inside a functioning operation.

Then the organization can make its long-term quarterback decision with a season of direct evidence.

### Staff

Aaron Kromer is probably the cleanest offensive coordinator target for this particular job.

Stone is already bringing his own passing-game and play-calling background.

What he needs beside him is someone capable of taking ownership of the line, run-game structure and day-to-day offensive operation.

That is a complementary hire rather than hiring another version of Stone.

Defensively, the first call is Rod Marinelli.

Not "Rod is staying."

Not "Rod has agreed."

Stone asks him.

If Marinelli believes he can work for Stone and wants to remain, continuity has real value.

If Marinelli does not want that arrangement, Stone does not hold him hostage because preserving the old defense requires the old coordinator.

Then Stone moves to the defensive board.

Crennel is a credible outside option.

### Emery relationship

Phil Emery should get a very detailed organizational answer because that is clearly part of what Chicago is evaluating.

Stone keeps play-calling, but he cannot spend Wednesday afternoon buried exclusively in third-down calls while the rest of the football team operates without its head coach.

The offensive coordinator owns substantial weekday administration.

The defensive coordinator runs the defensive room.

The special-teams coordinator runs the technical special-teams operation.

Stone sets the weekly priorities, controls the full practice schedule, makes final depth-chart and game-management decisions, oversees situational preparation, and makes sure the three phases are working toward the same game.

When the offensive plan is finalized, Stone owns the call sheet.

That is how he intends to remain the play-caller without becoming an absentee head coach.

### Year-one standard

Chicago should be trying to win immediately.

Stone should not ask a ten-win team for rebuilding patience.

But "win immediately" is not the same thing as telling Emery that anything short of eleven wins means failure.

The football objectives are clearer:

Keep the defense functioning.

Improve offensive operation.

Improve protection.

Get more consistency from the quarterback position.

Determine whether Cutler should be part of the next contract cycle.

Make the Bears a harder team to defend without weakening the side of the roster that already works.

That is a legitimate first-year assignment.

# Common operating framework

## Practice

Stone's practices should be organized around the week's actual work rather than arbitrary toughness.

The team practices football situations repeatedly.

Third down.

Red zone.

Backed up.

Two-minute.

Four-minute.

Goal line.

End-of-half management.

Ball security.

Special-teams transitions.

There should be physical periods when physical work is required.

There should not be unnecessary contact simply so the head coach can advertise that practice was hard.

Players need enough repetition to play quickly on Sunday.

## Player development

Position coaches are teachers first.

Young players need specific developmental plans.

What can the player do now?

What is preventing him from playing more?

What technique or assignment is being worked on?

How will the staff know whether he improved?

That process belongs in position meetings and practice tape, not in motivational speeches.

## Analytics and research

Stone uses information that helps make football decisions.

Fourth-down situations.

Clock management.

Opponent tendencies.

Self-scout.

Personnel efficiency.

Formation tendencies.

Protection tendencies.

Anything that gives the staff better information belongs in the conversation.

Stone does not outsource the decision to a spreadsheet.

He also does not ignore useful information because "football people have always done it this way."

The staff supplies the information.

Stone makes the decision.

## Discipline

Rules need to be understandable and consistently enforced.

Stone does not need fifty rules.

Be on time.

Prepare.

Practice correctly.

Do your job.

Do not hurt the football team.

When a player violates something, the consequence should depend on what actually happened, not his position on the depth chart.

## Media

Stone handles football issues directly.

He does not publicly blame assistants or players to protect himself.

He does not disclose internal personnel disagreements merely because a reporter asks.

When the organization has made a decision, Stone explains the football reasoning he can legitimately explain and moves on.

## Game management

Game-management decisions are prepared during the week.

Fourth-down ranges.

Timeout priorities.

End-of-half scenarios.

Two-point situations.

Challenge responsibilities.

Who is feeding Stone information.

What happens if the communication system fails.

Stone should not be figuring out that structure with forty seconds left in a game.

# Saints bounty due diligence

This answer has one hard boundary.

Current canon does not establish exactly when Stone first learned about the bounty program.

So do not manufacture that answer during an interview simulation.

The portion Stone can answer without changing canon is:

"I was New Orleans' offensive coordinator during the period the league investigated. Gregg Williams ran the defense. The league did not discipline me in connection with the bounty matter. I'm not going to sit here and give you somebody else's account of what happened. Ask me about my responsibilities and my conduct and I'll answer you directly.

If I am the head coach here, there is no gray area in the standard. Paying players for injuring opponents has no place in the program. If something violates league rules or crosses that line, it gets stopped. Being on the coaching staff does not exempt anybody from that standard."

If the club follows with:

"When did you personally first know?"

Stop there.

Stone's answer to that question requires an actual canon decision about his knowledge.

Do not evade it with some polished speech.

Do not invent that he knew nothing.

Do not invent that he knew everything.

Do not invent that he confronted Gregg Williams.

Do not invent that he reported it.

Do not invent that he concealed it.

That factual question remains unresolved until Stone's private timeline is actually established.
