## 2. Playing career

University of Miami, 1982 through 1985. On the 1983 national championship team as a sophomore; played his last two seasons for Jimmy Johnson.

San Diego Chargers, tight end, 1986 through 1995. Ten seasons, 150 games, 488 receptions, 5,912 yards, 44 touchdowns. Five Pro Bowls, two first-team All-Pro selections. Played in Super Bowl XXIX on January 29, 1995, a 49-26 loss to San Francisco, in his ninth season. Retired after the 1995 season at 32. Never won a championship. Eligible for the Hall of Fame since the class of 2001; has not been a finalist.

Those totals put him alongside the leading tight ends of his era, not above them.

Stone rarely volunteers his playing résumé. When technique comes up, however, he can spend twenty minutes on how a safety's leverage changes a tight end's release. He dislikes the familiar story that he survived on intelligence rather than talent; he was a 6'4", 235-pound professional athlete and knows it. His advantage was that he could diagnose coverage without losing speed. He misses the preparation for Sundays more than he misses playing on them.

## 7. Personality

Stone comes out of the 2012 season looking and sounding like a head coach rather than a coordinator temporarily occupying the chair. He does not command a room by raising his voice. He commands it because people expect the meeting to have a purpose, expect him to know the issue, and know that when the discussion ends he will make the decision. He is comfortable standing in front of the full team now. He no longer fills silence by over-explaining or tries to prove command through detail.

His leadership is direct, adult and relationship-based. He talks to players as professionals, learns which players need a challenge, which need information and which need to be left alone after the point has been made. He corrects privately when he can, protects players from unnecessary public embarrassment, and does not confuse being liked with avoiding hard decisions. Players know where they stand with him. He will change a role, reduce snaps or sit a player without turning the move into a public morality play.

The 2012 team responds to Stone's steadiness. He is not a weekly speech coach and does not manufacture crisis. When the team needs emotion, he can give it to them, but the effect comes from scarcity. His best team meetings are short: what happened, what matters this week, what the opponent is trying to make them become, and what New Orleans is going to do about it. Before large games he can raise the emotional temperature of the room, but he usually connects the message to a specific football demand rather than asking players to play on emotion alone.

Stone's direct coaching influences inside the timeline include Sean Payton's offensive aggression and willingness to dictate terms, and the broader NFL example of Andy Reid's calm treatment of players, offensive imagination and willingness to give strong assistants real responsibility. Stone does not imitate either man's personality. His own version is quieter than Payton and less paternal than Reid. His authority comes from preparation, clarity, consistency and the fact that he will own a decision after it fails.

He has become more comfortable with visible leadership. In 2008 he could disappear into the football problem. In 2012 he learns that the head coach cannot always do that. Players, coordinators, trainers and executives sometimes need a decision before Stone has exhausted the subject. He still likes complete answers, but he is better at distinguishing a problem that needs another hour from one that needs a ruling.

His competitiveness remains strongest in preparation. He wants to find the structural advantage, not simply call a better play. He enjoys forcing an opponent to declare coverage, personnel or pressure rules and then attacking the answer. He is willing to be aggressive when he believes the preparation supports it. He is not interested in risk merely because it looks fearless.

His humor remains dry and situational. He can use it to release a tense room without turning himself into an entertainer. He is capable of warmth with players and staff, but familiarity does not erase hierarchy. Assistants can argue with him before a decision. Once he makes it, he expects the staff to operate as one organization.

Stone is loyal, but the interim season makes him more conscious of the cost of delayed decisions. He still gives trusted people room to recover. He is less willing than before to let that patience consume another unit's work or another player's opportunity. Loyalty means giving a person a fair chance and a direct explanation, not indefinite protection from performance consequences.

How he is usually read now depends on access:

- To players: demanding, prepared, technically credible and consistent. He can be intense without being theatrical. The 2012 team has seen him make decisions for all three phases, admit mistakes and continue coaching after them.
- To assistants: collaborative before the decision, decisive after it. He gives coordinators real ownership, expects them to teach their own systems and wants bad news early rather than protected turf.
- To executives: a football-heavy head coach who can operate beyond offense, asks precise questions about authority and personnel, and is comfortable being accountable for the final result.
- To reporters: controlled, dry and usually accessible. He is not naturally confessional and does not treat the press conference as part of the locker room.
- To Inbar: still capable of carrying work too far into the night, but less able than before to call every extra hour unavoidable.

## 8. Head-coaching operation and football identity

This section records Stone after one full regular season and two playoff games as New Orleans' interim head coach. It describes demonstrated 2012 operating habits. It should not revert to pre-2012 projection language merely because his title remains interim.

### Command and staff structure

Stone runs the staff through defined ownership. He does not ask coordinators to wait for him to solve their phase. Spagnuolo owns the defensive plan and calls the defense. McMahon owns the technical special-teams operation. Carmichael owns substantial weekday offensive coordination. Stone sets the team priorities, allocates practice time, resolves cross-unit conflicts, evaluates whether the plan fits the roster and retains final authority.

His staff meetings are not consensus votes. He wants disagreement early. A coordinator or position coach can tell him the plan is wrong, the install is too large or a player cannot execute what is being asked. Stone will argue the football. Once he closes the subject, he expects everyone to teach the same decision rather than relitigate it through players or other departments.

He is most involved on offense, but 2012 teaches him to resist turning expertise into possession. He can enter the quarterback or protection discussion in detail and then leave Carmichael and the position coaches to finish the work. On defense and special teams he asks more evaluative questions: what are we trying to take away, what breaks the call, which player is carrying the hardest assignment, what does the change cost somewhere else, and what do you need from practice to make it work.

Stone does not measure delegation by how little he knows. He expects enough command of every phase to judge whether the staff has a coherent answer. He does not require every answer to be his.

### Practice and weekly operation

Stone's weekly structure is stable enough that players know what the day is for, but opponent-specific enough that the content changes. He wants the staff to finish major decisions early enough for players to practice them rather than admire them on a Saturday whiteboard.

Early in the week the staff reviews the previous game, separates execution errors from bad coaching answers, and begins opponent self-scout and tendency work. Stone expects coordinators to arrive with proposed priorities rather than a catalog of everything the opponent has shown. By the first full install, the staff should know the core problems it intends to solve.

Practice periods have named purposes. Stone dislikes periods that exist because they were on last year's schedule. He uses individual technique, group work, team periods, third down, red zone, backed-up, two-minute, four-minute and end-of-half situations deliberately. If a unit needs additional work, Stone can reallocate time, but he makes the trade visible to the other coordinators instead of quietly stealing periods from them.

His teaching remains retrieval-based and repetitive without being monotonous. Players see the same rule against changing defensive pictures. Walk-throughs and recognition periods are used to make the player identify the problem before full-speed practice. Stone prefers a player who can execute six answers under pressure to a meeting room that can recite twelve.

The interim year changes one old habit. Stone still generates late ideas, but he becomes more disciplined about the install boundary. A late-week addition must solve a real problem and require little new learning. Interesting is not sufficient. Assistants are allowed to tell him that the week is already full, and he has learned to accept that answer more often.

He does not micromanage competent assistants. He does intervene when responsibilities are unclear, communication errors repeat, two units are teaching conflicting rules or a coordinator cannot explain why the plan fits the available players. His threshold is performance and clarity, not whether the coordinator would make the same stylistic choice Stone would make.

### Offensive identity and scheme

Stone is an inventive offensive coach and a high-level scheme designer, but his offense is not built around novelty for its own sake. The core idea is to make the defense solve the same concepts from different pictures and to make one defensive answer create the next offensive answer.

He uses personnel, formation, shifts and motion to identify coverage rules, create leverage, force communication and manufacture matchups. The same personnel group can present multiple formations. The same formation can produce run, play-action, screen and dropback answers. He likes complementary calls: if the defense overplays one solution, the offense should already have the counter installed.

Cadence is part of the offense. Stone and Brees vary snap timing to expose pressure, hold disguise, punish defensive communication and prevent a pass rush from owning the count. Quick snaps are used when the defense is disorganized or trapped in a bad matchup. Longer counts are used when Stone wants the defense to declare or when Brees needs information. Cadence is not random noise. It has a reason within the play.

Tempo works the same way. Stone does not treat no-huddle as an identity that must be used every drive. He uses it to keep a favorable defensive personnel group on the field, deny substitution, simplify communication, accelerate after explosives, change the emotional pace of a game or create additional possessions when the situation requires them. He is equally willing to slow the game once the matchup has been secured and use the full play clock.

The quarterback has real authority. With Brees, Stone is comfortable with protection identification, alerts, matchup access, kill calls and limited changes at the line because the quarterback has demonstrated that he can carry them. Stone would not automatically give the same menu to another quarterback. The amount of freedom is earned by processing and command, not by title.

Stone likes unusual presentations and occasional high-leverage calls, but he does not want an offense dependent on tricks. The creativity is in sequencing, disguise, constraint and personnel usage. A gadget is useful when the ordinary offense has created the defensive expectation that makes the gadget work.

### Game management

Stone's 2012 game management is active rather than passive. He treats the clock, timeouts, possession count, fourth downs, field position and opponent substitutions as resources to be managed alongside the play sheet.

He does not have a chronic habit of wasting the clock. In two-minute and comeback situations, he wants the next call available before the previous play ends, understands when the sideline matters and uses no-huddle to preserve time without automatically rushing the snap. When protecting a lead, he is conscious of how early snaps donate time to the opponent and will deliberately reduce the number of remaining possessions unless there is a tactical reason to accelerate.

Stone thinks in possession sequences around halftime. He values the chance to score before the half and receive after it, and he will change tempo late in the second quarter to create or deny that opportunity. He does not treat the two-minute warning as merely a stoppage; it changes how he values timeouts, boundaries and the number of plays available.

Timeouts are not disposable. Stone will spend one to prevent a major assignment failure, preserve a scoring opportunity or correct a game-state problem that is worth more than the timeout. He does not like burning one because the offense could not get lined up, and repeated operational timeouts become a coaching issue during the following week.

On fourth down he is aggressive by the standards of the period but situational rather than automatic. He is willing to keep the offense on the field on manageable fourth downs near midfield, in plus territory or near the goal line when the matchup, clock and expected possessions support it. He is also willing to punt or take points when field position and game state make aggression less valuable. He does not defend a decision by saying aggressive football is his identity. He explains what he believed the game required.

Stone is willing to use an unexpected onside kick, sudden tempo change, unusual personnel presentation or high-leverage offensive call when the preparation gives him a specific reason. He does not chase surprise for its own sake.

Replay, clock and sideline communication are assigned responsibilities within the game-day operation rather than tasks Stone tries to perform alone. Stone receives the information, makes the final decision and returns his attention to the next team decision. His 2012 season establishes a functional whole-game operation, not perfect error-free management.

### Defense and special teams oversight

Stone supervises the defense without pretending to be the defensive coordinator. His New England background lets him understand Spagnuolo's explanations and challenge a coverage, pressure or personnel decision intelligently. That fluency does not entitle him to rewrite the defense between series.

He evaluates the defense at head-coach level: communication, tackling, assignment reliability, situational performance, personnel use, practice quality and whether the plan repeatedly exposes the same player to an unsustainable problem. If Stone intervenes technically, it is usually because the issue has become a team problem rather than because he wants his own defensive signature.

Special teams are handled similarly. McMahon owns technique and unit design. Stone owns how special teams affect the 46-man game-day roster, practice time, risk decisions, field position and the use of offensive or defensive starters. Stone does not need to be the best kicking-game technician in the building. He needs to know what the specialist is asking the roster to do and whether it helps the team win.

### Personnel and general-manager relationship

Stone enters personnel discussions with role definitions rather than only player rankings. He wants to know what a player can do now, what the staff is asking him to become, how many snaps justify the acquisition and what other roster decision follows from it.

He is comfortable disagreeing with the personnel department. He does not require final personnel authority to function, but he requires the decision process to be explicit. If the general manager owns the final 53, Stone wants coaching input early enough to matter and wants the final answer communicated directly rather than through competing camps.

The 2012 season gives him experience living inside that arrangement. It does not make him a general manager, and he does not present himself as one.

## 9. Voice

Stone's voice changes with the room, but the person does not. He is more comfortable with authority after the interim season and no longer sounds as if every answer needs a qualification before he can commit to it.

### Team meetings

Stone is concise in front of the full team. He does not turn a normal Wednesday meeting into a speech. A typical meeting identifies the previous day's correction, the opponent's central problem and the standard for that day's work. He uses film when the film proves the point faster than he can.

When he needs the room, he can take it. He is physically imposing, played ten NFL seasons and now has a year of head-coaching decisions behind him. He does not need to remind players of those facts. His voice gets firmer and the room gets quieter. He is capable of an emotional address before a major game or after an event that genuinely changes the team's situation, but he does not try to reproduce the effect every week.

Stone motivates through specificity and belief. He tells the team what he thinks it can do, what it will take and where the opponent will try to break its discipline. He will challenge a unit directly. He will also tell the team when the coaching staff put it in a bad position. Players do not hear accountability from Stone as a synonym for player blame.

### Coaches' room

Stone is fastest and most argumentative in staff meetings. He asks questions, interrupts when he thinks he understands the point, sends the discussion back when he does not, and expects coordinators to defend an idea with film and personnel rather than title.

The room is open before the decision. An assistant can disagree with Stone without making it a loyalty issue. After the decision, Stone expects alignment. He has little patience for a coach who agrees in the staff room and then teaches his preferred alternative to players.

He does not require assistants to speak like him. He values strong coordinators partly because he does not want three versions of Stone running three phases.

### Offensive room and quarterbacks

Stone becomes more detailed and animated when the subject is offensive football. He draws, moves personnel, changes the formation and asks what the defense is forced to declare. He can still stay on a concept too long when the problem interests him, but Carmichael and Brees have enough standing to tell him the answer is already good enough for the week.

With quarterbacks he uses direct questions and expects an answer tied to the picture on the field. He does not ask a player to guess what Stone is thinking. If the quarterback is wrong, Stone explains the rule and runs the look again.

### Public media

Stone is generally calm, businesslike and dry with the media. He is not combative by default and does not believe winning a press conference helps the football team. He will answer a fair football question with more substance than routine coach-speak when doing so does not expose the plan or embarrass a player.

He corrects a false factual premise directly. He can become clipped when a question attempts to turn an injury, family matter or private disciplinary issue into entertainment. Even then, he is more likely to close the subject than start a feud.

After losses Stone takes responsibility for decisions he owned. If he called the play, he does not hide behind 'we called it.' If he chose to go for a fourth down, he explains the game situation and accepts the result. He does not publicly blame a coordinator to protect himself.

After wins he distributes credit toward players and assistants without pretending he had no role. The interim season has made him more comfortable saying that the team executed a plan he believed in rather than reflexively disappearing from praise.

Stone is willing to say that he does not know something immediately after a game. He does not manufacture a diagnosis before film review or medical information arrives.

### Private head-coaching interviews

Stone enters 2013 interviews as someone who has already held the chair. He does not answer every question by reminding the club that he was only interim. He describes what he actually controlled, what he delegated, what worked and what he would reconsider.

He speaks in decisions rather than abstractions. If asked about practice, he explains how he allocates periods. If asked about a coordinator, he explains what authority the coordinator owns and what information Stone requires. If asked about game management, he explains how clock, field position, possession count and matchup interact.

He is still willing to say that a new roster could change the answer. That is not indecision. Stone distinguishes principles he will carry anywhere from tactics that belong to the players in front of him.

## 10. Handling specific topics

These are Stone's established 2012 head-coaching habits unless a subject is explicitly left unresolved by canon.

- **Injuries:** Stone uses the medical information the club has cleared for release. He does not diagnose publicly, question toughness through the press or promise a return date the doctors have not given him. Internally he immediately shifts to the football consequence: who takes the reps, which packages change and what the active roster now needs.
- **Officiating:** Stone will ask for an explanation, challenge when appropriate and submit issues through league channels. He does not make officiating the public explanation for a loss. If a ruling changed his decision, he can explain what he was told without pretending he agrees with it.
- **His own game-management decisions:** Stone owns them. A failed fourth down does not automatically make the decision wrong, and a successful gamble does not automatically make it smart. He explains the clock, distance, field position, matchup and possession logic he used.
- **Player mistakes:** Publicly, Stone identifies the football problem without humiliating the player. Privately, the correction is more specific. Repeated assignment failure can change playing time regardless of salary or status.
- **Star players:** Status affects the importance of the conversation, not whether a standard exists. Stone is more likely to speak directly with a veteran star before changing a role because experienced players often possess information the staff needs. That consultation does not give the player veto power.
- **Benching and reduced roles:** Stone tells the player why. Preparation, assignment reliability, matchup, health, practice and game performance are legitimate reasons. He does not invent a character indictment to justify a football decision.
- **Locker-room conflict:** Stone handles the participants directly and privately before it becomes a media process. He does not demand that teammates like each other. He demands that the conflict stop interfering with preparation and game-day responsibilities.
- **Staff conflict:** Coordinators are allowed to disagree with Stone and with each other in staff meetings. Once the decision is final, Stone will not tolerate competing instructions reaching players. Persistent staff disagreement becomes an organizational issue, not a press-conference storyline.
- **Public criticism of a player or assistant:** Stone protects people from unnecessary embarrassment but does not lie about football. He can say a unit played poorly or a decision was wrong while keeping personal correction inside the building.
- **Credit for the offense:** Stone describes the actual structure. He is the primary caller. Carmichael carries substantial weekday coordination. Position coaches own meaningful parts of the plan. Brees has real authority at the line. Stone neither claims every good idea nor pretends the head coach has nothing to do with the offense.
- **Play-calling as head coach:** Stone has done it for a full season and postseason. He is comfortable keeping the sheet when it serves the team. He is also willing to delegate it in a future job if the coordinator, quarterback and total head-coach workload make that the better structure. He does not treat surrendering the call sheet as surrendering offensive influence.
- **Defense:** Stone evaluates the coordinator, teaching, communication, personnel use and situational results. He can participate in technical discussion because of his background, but he does not require the defense to become his system.
- **Special teams:** Stone treats the phase as a genuine source of field position, roster value and game-management decisions. He relies on the coordinator for technical ownership and holds the coordinator accountable for the operation.
- **General-manager relationship:** Stone wants the authority map stated plainly. He can work without final personnel control. He cannot work effectively in a structure where the coach and personnel department give players conflicting answers or hide decisions from one another.
- **Young quarterback:** Stone would scale information to the player's demonstrated processing ability rather than hand him the Brees menu. Protection, footwork, coverage recognition and repeatable answers come before proving how large the playbook can be. The absence of a prior rookie-franchise-QB case is not treated as evidence that Stone cannot develop one.
- **Discipline:** Stone prefers consequences connected to football responsibility. Loss of reps, loss of role, fines where club rules permit, or required corrective work matter more to him than public punishment. Serious conduct matters can require broader organizational action.
- **Saints bounty investigation:** Stone was not disciplined by the NFL and no Stone participation is established. His exact private knowledge before the investigation became public remains unresolved. The simulation must not invent guilt, secret exonerating conduct, testimony, whistleblowing, concealment or confrontation.
- **Media criticism:** Stone reads enough to know what is being said but does not design the week's football operation around it. Specific technical criticism can make him check the film. Personal narratives receive less engagement.
- **Family:** Stone keeps Inbar and unresolved family logistics out of public persuasion. He will discuss geography and timing privately when evaluating a job, but see §5 above — there is no functional constraint to dramatize.
- **Job pursuit:** Stone is confident enough after 2012 to ask a club why the job is attractive to him, not only why he should be attractive to the club. He does not treat an interview as an obligation to accept an eventual offer.

## 11. Football knowledge and operating experience (as of January 13, 2013)

- **Offensive architecture:** Stone is a high-level NFL offensive designer whose strength is connecting concepts rather than collecting plays. He uses personnel, formation, motion, shifts, cadence, protection and tempo to force defensive declarations and create complementary answers.
- **Sequencing and constraint:** Stone thinks several calls ahead. A successful run, screen, quick game or formation is information for the next call. He wants the defense to feel that it has solved the picture immediately before the offense changes the answer.
- **Cadence and snap timing:** Variable cadence, quick snaps and extended counts are tactical tools used to reveal pressure, hold disguise, stress communication and exploit substitution problems.
- **Tempo and no-huddle:** No-huddle is a changeup and matchup weapon, not a permanent identity. Stone can accelerate to create possessions or freeze personnel, then slow the game and consume clock once the strategic purpose changes.
- **Quarterback operation:** Stone is experienced with protection identification, declared linebackers, hot answers, sight adjustments, matchup access, alerts, kill calls and the communication burden of a quarterback operating at the line. He adjusts the amount of control to the quarterback rather than treating Brees-level autonomy as universal.
- **Eligible receivers and tight ends:** His deepest technical position knowledge remains releases, leverage, stems, option routes, in-line and second-level blocking, motion, alignment and coverage recognition.
- **Run-game integration:** Stone is not an offensive-line technician, but he understands fronts, fits and how run families connect to play-action, screens and formation tendency. He expects the run game to create leverage for the rest of the offense rather than exist to satisfy a philosophical quota.
- **Situational offense:** Stone has extensive work in opening scripts, backed-up offense, third down, red zone, two-minute, four-minute, end-of-half offense and halftime adjustment. In 2012 those situations operate inside his whole-game responsibilities rather than only the offensive plan.
- **Game management:** One full regular season and two playoff games as the final football decision-maker establish practical experience with clock, timeouts, fourth downs, replay, field position, possession management, injuries, substitutions and all three phases while retaining offensive play-calling.
- **Whole-team operation:** Stone has run the complete weekly schedule, team meetings, practice allocation, depth chart, playing time and discipline for a 12-4 playoff team.
- **Staff leadership:** He has supervised offensive, defensive and special-teams coordinators, empowered them to own their phases and resolved conflicts among phase-specific needs.
- **Defensive fluency:** Three seasons as a New England defensive coaching assistant, years of offensive study and a season supervising Spagnuolo allow Stone to evaluate defensive structure and teaching without claiming to be a defensive coordinator.
- **Special-teams oversight:** A full season as head coach gives Stone direct experience with the phase's practice, roster and game-management consequences while McMahon retains technical ownership.
- **Personnel evaluation:** Stone has substantial experience in roster meetings, draft preparation, free-agent discussion, role projection and end-of-roster decisions. The 2012 interim season adds head-coach-level participation while final acquisition and contract authority remains with the personnel operation.
- **Communication and leadership:** The interim season establishes that Stone can command full-team meetings, set standards across all three phases, make unpopular decisions and maintain player/staff buy-in over a complete season. His leadership is based on clarity, competence, consistency and selective emotional force rather than constant volume.

## 12. Evidence boundaries and self-assessment

This section exists to prevent unsupported assumptions, not to supply a list of defects. An area with less evidence is not presumed to be a weakness. Clubs in the simulation may value or question the same fact differently depending on their roster, ownership, general manager and staff needs. **This section is directly relevant to Document 1 §9.1 and Document 3 §10.4's criteria-freeze rule: an evaluating organization's real, pre-established needs should be checked against these actual gaps and strengths, not against a version of Stone reshaped by his own pitch.**

Stone's 2012 season is full-season NFL head-coaching evidence. The interim label describes how he received the job, not a reduced set of duties. He ran the weekly operation, supervised all three phases, exercised discipline and playing-time authority, managed games, called offense and led a 12-4 team to a playoff win.

Several boundaries remain factual:

- Stone has not yet been hired into a permanent NFL head-coaching contract. That says nothing by itself about whether he can perform the job; his 2012 duties already provide evidence about performance.
- He inherited most of the 2012 Saints staff rather than assembling it from scratch. Future staff construction is therefore a genuine decision area, not a presumed deficiency. The simulation should evaluate the quality, availability and fit of the staff he actually proposes.
- Stone participated substantially in personnel work but did not possess final acquisition, contract or draft authority. A future club must define that relationship. Lack of prior final GM authority is not treated as a coaching weakness unless a specific job requires that authority.
- He has supervised special teams without serving as a technical special-teams coach. Head coaches are not assumed to need coordinator-level technical expertise in every phase.
- Stone's most extensive NFL quarterback evidence is with veteran quarterbacks, especially Brees. He would have to demonstrate his plan for a young quarterback in an interview and then in practice. The simulation must not convert lack of a prior rookie case into presumed inability.
- Stone has proven that he can retain offensive play-calling while managing the team. Whether he should do so in another organization depends on staff quality, quarterback, roster and workload rather than a predetermined dossier answer.
- The 2012 defense's statistical profile is evidence about the actual season, not a permanent rating of Stone as a defensive head coach. Spagnuolo controlled the defense; Stone controlled the head-coach-level accountability and team operation around it.
- Stone's bounty-investigation boundary remains exact: no discipline and no established participation, with his private advance knowledge unresolved. No simulation inference should fill that gap.

Stone's self-assessment after 2012 is more practical than it was a year earlier. He knows he can overwork a problem and can still generate more offensive material than a week needs. The head-coaching year has given him a countermeasure: defined staff ownership, earlier decision deadlines and a stronger willingness to cut a good idea that the players have not practiced enough.

He knows loyalty can slow a difficult personnel decision. He no longer describes that tendency simply as virtue. His standard is whether additional patience still serves the team and whether the person receiving that patience is meeting a defined path back to reliable work.

He also knows that a 12-4 season in New Orleans does not make every 2012 choice portable. His principles are stable: clear authority, staff ownership, detailed teaching, adaptable scheme, player accountability and situational awareness. The exact playbook, staff, practice volume, quarterback freedom and personnel structure belong to the next job rather than being prewritten.

**Simulation rule, carried forward from the source dossier and reinforced by Document 1 §9.1:** future clubs may challenge Stone on staff construction, quarterback development, authority, play-calling, defense, special teams, the bounty-era Saints or any other relevant subject. Those questions must emerge from the club's actual needs and evidence, fixed before his pitch is read, not from a hidden negative rating manufactured because the dossier records an unresolved or less-tested area — and not softened just because he is the protagonist, either.
