# Alex Stone: ownership review

*Proposed editorial reconstruction from ownership's perspective, using the career through September 28, 2014. This is a draft assessment, not a new statement by Shad Khan, a meeting or an employment decision. The January 15, 2014 retention remains the club's last recorded employment action.*

We kept Alex under his existing contract after the 2013 season. The first four games of 2014 support continuing with him. The club is winning, the quarterback has made progress and the turnover numbers are better. Opponents are also converting half their third downs, and Alex has added defensive calling to his responsibilities. Our confidence in him has a football basis. The remaining questions concern how much of this improvement will hold and whether his staff is getting all of its work done.

## The job we hired him to do

We hired Alex Stone on January 15, 2013, after a two-win season. We had changed general managers, needed a coaching staff and had no settled answer at quarterback. Before we could reasonably expect this team to contend, we needed a football operation that could decide what it had, teach its players and carry a week's preparation into Sunday. Those were substantial obligations in their own right. They were the standard at the appointment, which carried no immediate-playoff requirement.

Stone's New Orleans experience gave us reason to believe he could manage more than an offensive meeting room. He had been offensive coordinator since 2009 and took over the primary offensive calls during the 2011 season. On April 16, 2012, he became interim head coach while Sean Payton remained the permanent head coach under suspension. Stone ran the team schedule, meetings, practice allocation, playing time and game management while continuing to call the offense. Carmichael carried substantial weekday passing-game work, Kromer the line and running game, Spagnuolo the defense and McMahon special teams. Personnel authority remained elsewhere.

That Saints team went 12-4, won a Wild Card game and lost in the Divisional round on January 13, 2013. Stone had kept an established contender functioning through a difficult interruption. He inherited Drew Brees, an experienced staff and a team coming off 13-3. Jacksonville would require him to build much more of the operation himself. His New Orleans season was relevant evidence for the hire, with a different starting point from the one we were giving him here.

Our 2012 Jaguars had scored 255 points and allowed 444. They converted fewer than three in ten third downs, allowed 50 sacks and produced 20. Gabbert and Henne had both started, and neither had settled the position. Injuries had complicated the work on offense. There were useful players to build around, including Jones-Drew, Monroe, Lewis, Shorts and Blackmon. We owed Stone an honest assessment of those players and their availability, just as he owed us an honest assessment of what his staff could do with them. Calling the roster hopeless would excuse too much; assuming the existing talent should immediately produce a winning team would ignore what had happened.

The authority arrangement was part of our commitment. Stone selected his staff and controlled preparation, the depth chart and game decisions. Caldwell retained scouting, acquisitions, contracts, the cap and the draft. Major quarterback commitments required Stone's written evaluation and consultation, with Caldwell holding the final decision. We accepted a four-year, fully guaranteed contract and a staff-funding arrangement consistent with the proposed staff. If the roster proved inadequate, ownership could not fairly treat every deficiency as a coaching failure after approving that division of responsibility. ## What the first season changed

Jacksonville went from 2-14 to 10-6. It finished second in the AFC South on the division-record tiebreaker, entered the playoffs as the fifth seed and won 38-14 at Kansas City. A 20-13 loss at Tennessee ended the season. We had meaningful games in December and January, including a one-point Week 17 loss to Indianapolis that cost us the division. That was a considerable change in what the club was capable of playing for.

The improvement extended beyond the standings. We scored 356 points, 101 more than the year before, and allowed 346, 98 fewer. The scoring margin improved from minus-189 to plus-10. The offense gained 989 more net yards; the defense allowed 739 fewer. Third-down conversion rose from 29.6 to 39.6 percent, while opponents' conversion rate fell from 41.4 to 33.7 percent. A team that had struggled to sustain possessions and get off the field became substantially better at both.

The quarterback decision illustrates why credit has to include the front office and the coaches. Caldwell acquired Kirk Cousins for a 2014 second-round pick and traded Gabbert to Green Bay for C.J. Wilson. Stone conducted the competition, selected the starter and took responsibility for how the offense used him. Cousins started throughout the season and supplied 3,981 passing yards and 23 touchdowns. The acquisition created an option; the coaching staff had to turn that option into a working offense. Each part of that sequence belonged to a different job.

Young players received consequential work. Lane Johnson started at right tackle. Kelce, Poyer, Sio Moore and Rambo held roles, and Thielen became WR2 after arriving as an undrafted player. That matters because development was part of the assignment we gave Stone. Playing time is the beginning of that assessment. The stronger evidence would be players understanding more, executing more reliably and earning additional responsibility as the year went on.

Stone also got through the season with the staff he assembled. Tice coordinated the offensive week, Stone called the offense and Crennel coordinated and called the defense. The January review found that the Caldwell-Stone division worked. A useful partnership allows the coach to challenge a personnel evaluation and the GM to challenge a request without making every disagreement an ownership matter. Their disagreements were being handled without repeatedly reaching ownership.

Those results justified continuing with him. They also changed the next assessment. The two-win season explains the scale of the first improvement; it cannot remain the standard forever. Once this team has shown it can reach the playoffs, we need to understand what would allow it to return and compete there more convincingly. ## The football that still needs an answer

Ten wins came with a scoring margin of only ten points. Tennessee beat us 41-11, Houston 38-6 and Buffalo 45-16. We committed six turnovers in the first of those games and four in the last. Buffalo rushed for 237 yards. These were afternoons when an early problem became several problems and the team could not recover. The coaching response during those games deserves examination alongside the players' mistakes. Three defeats of that size are too much of the season to dismiss as occasional bad football.

The offense finished 12th in scoring and 15th in net yards. It could function and win games, but the quarterback's 18 interceptions and 38 sacks left a substantial part of the work unfinished. Protection improved from 50 sacks allowed in 2012, yet 38 still ranked tied 25th in the completed 2013 comparison. Our investment in a young quarterback carries an obligation to understand the pressure he is under and the decisions he is making before we decide what he can become.

Ownership cannot identify the responsible player from a sack total. The football staff should be able to explain which failures came from the protection identification, a block, a delayed throw or a call that demanded too much time. That explanation has consequences. Repeated physical losses may require Caldwell to improve the line. Repeated identification mistakes may require different teaching. If the caller keeps asking for a throw the protection cannot support, Stone has to adjust. We need those distinctions before committing more resources or declaring the quarterback the problem.

Houston left a more direct question for Stone. Jones-Drew gained 86 yards on 12 carries, while the team finished with 17 rushes and 45 dropbacks in a 38-6 loss. Stone later acknowledged that he had stopped calling what was working. That admission was useful. What matters afterward is whether it changes his response when another game begins moving away from him. The staff needs a way to recognize an effective part of the offense, preserve it when the score permits and challenge the caller before the game is gone. An explanation in January does not tell us whether that exchange happened on the next difficult Sunday.

Crennel's defense made a major contribution. Fifth in net yards allowed and third on third down are substantial strengths. The same defense finished 31st in sacks and 31st in takeaways, with team points allowed tied 15th. It was much better at restricting movement than at creating additional possessions. Our takeaways fell from 23 to 15 while giveaways fell only from 26 to 25. The turnover differential therefore worsened from minus-3 to minus-10 during a season in which most other team measures improved.

The explanation belongs with Caldwell, Crennel and Stone together. How often was pressure arriving too late? Were the players capable of winning their matchups? Were coverage choices limiting opportunities, or were opportunities being missed? We should expect a coherent account from the people who watch every snap, including what can be coached and what requires a different player. Our responsibility is to test whether their account fits the evidence and their proposed remedy. We need to hear where they agree and where their evaluations differ.

Special teams need the same attention even when the season summary gives us less information. At Houston, a 26-yard punt return and a zero-yard punt downed at the line of scrimmage preceded touchdown possessions. Both failures gave Houston better field position. Before judging the coordinator's season, we need the rest of the kicking, punting, coverage, return and protection work alongside those plays. Stone also shares responsibility through the game-day roster and the practice time available to that unit. A reserve who helps an offensive package may cost something if he displaces a dependable coverage player.

Game management belongs in this assessment when an actual choice is recorded. The relevant question is whether Stone understood the options, time, score, personnel and conditions at the moment. A missed kick alone does not settle whether attempting it was reasonable. [Houston review][houston] · [Quarterback review][cousins] · [Season statistics][sheet]

## Whether the building delivers what he promises

Stone's approach depends heavily on teaching. He wants players to understand the job well enough to ask questions and correct themselves. That can build confidence in a young team. It also creates a standard by which the players can judge him: when someone identifies a problem, does the staff make time to work on it, and does anyone check whether the work helped?

Thielen's individual development period disappeared after the bye. The unresolved point is whether the work had been completed or had simply lost its place to weekly preparation. For an undrafted receiver trying to keep a role, that distinction matters. The staff may reasonably change his schedule, but the player should understand what replaced the period and what he is still expected to improve. Otherwise a promise of development becomes something available only when the week is easy.

Kelce's work on attached blocking raised a related issue: the correction needed a clear completion standard. Cousins wanted paired, specific film that showed the problem and the intended answer. The players were identifying something the staff could act on. When we assess that response, the useful evidence is what each player could do afterward and how reliably he could repeat it.

There was a concrete response during the June 17-19 minicamp. Bates showed Cousins the requested end-zone view of a June 9 overload. Cousins identified the wrong-way slide and the correct HALF answer before being prompted, and the staff later retested him without the aid. That is evidence of teaching reaching the player. Signed players received their summer sheets on June 19; the film packets were not issued at that handoff. We would need the subsequent handoff to establish whether that remained an outstanding obligation in September.

Stone needs to know when promised work is being displaced and be able to get a reliable answer from the assistant responsible. He is responsible for that follow-up even when an assistant does the teaching. If players repeatedly have to raise the same unfinished issue at exit interviews, the head coach's approachable manner has not yet produced a dependable process. If the staff closes the issue and the player performs the task independently, the teaching deserves credit.

The same care applies to the staff. Respect for Stone can coexist with disagreement about preparation, player usage or workload. We should be wary of judging the building only by whether people sound supportive after a win. The harder test comes when an assistant raises an inconvenient football problem. Does Stone hear it, and does the assistant leave understanding the final decision? An assurance that everyone gets along does not answer either question.

Staff continuity now has a new test. Lowry left on January 12, 2014 to become Atlanta's head coach, after our elimination. Westhoff was appointed on February 11. That was a vacancy created by advancement, with a handoff to manage. Stone has to integrate the new coordinator while preserving useful knowledge about the roster and making room for changes Westhoff considers necessary. Over time, the quality of those transitions will tell us more about Stone's ability to run a program than the fact that his original staff completed one season together. ## What four games in 2014 tell us

We are 3-1 after September 28. Jacksonville won 23-20 in overtime at Philadelphia, 24-7 at Washington and 38-34 against Indianapolis, then lost 21-20 at San Diego. The team has scored 105 points and allowed 82. A plus-23 margin in four games is encouraging after finishing the previous regular season at plus-10, although four games cannot establish the consistency we are looking for.

There are improvements in the areas raised in January. Cousins has nine touchdown passes against four interceptions. The team has given the ball away five times and taken it away six, and it has allowed eight sacks while producing ten. San Diego was our first turnover-free game. That was welcome after last year's difficulties, even in a one-point loss. The improvement needs to hold against different opponents before we can be confident that the identification and decision work has taken.

Defense is a more immediate concern than the record might suggest. Opponents have converted 34 of 68 third downs, exactly half. Indianapolis produced three fourth-quarter touchdown drives. Those are significant difficulties for a team whose third-down defense was a strength in 2013. The football explanation should account for the downs and distances, personnel and execution involved; the total tells us the problem is recurring, not why it is happening.

Stone took over defensive calling for Week 4 under the arrangement established after Week 3. Crennel remains responsible for coordinating the defense, preparing it and transmitting the calls. Stone now carries both sets of calls. We need to understand whether that arrangement leaves him enough attention for the whole game: offensive adjustments, substitutions, communication between units and the next consequential clock or possession decision. Tice and Crennel's responsibilities become especially important when the head coach is occupied with the other side of the ball.

One game gives us little basis to judge the sustainability of the workload. Across the next several weeks, we should be able to see who is monitoring each part of the game while Stone calls the other side, how quickly information reaches him and whether the arrangement helps the defense. ## Our position on his job

We retained Stone on his existing terms on January 15, 2014, following the review with Caldwell. No extension, raise or change in authority was requested or offered. Caldwell identified scoring margin, quarterback ball security and protection as leading measures for the next season. There was no stated minimum win total or playoff ultimatum.

On the football available now, there is a sound case for continuing with Stone. He delivered a functioning first-year program, a playoff win and a workable relationship with the general manager. The second season has begun 3-1, with encouraging early movement in several of the areas identified for improvement. Nothing in those four games supplies a persuasive football case for reversing the retention decision. The defensive problems and the expanded calling workload deserve attention within that continuing relationship.

The four-year guarantee is part of our commitment to him. A coaching change would involve both a financial obligation and the disruption of replacing people and methods throughout the building. Those consequences require a considered decision. At present, continuing also has a strong football justification: the program has made substantial progress, and the staff has work in progress that deserves the opportunity to show a result.

Trust matters in that judgment. We need Stone and Caldwell to give us the same underlying account they give each other, including the uncomfortable parts. Stone's willingness to acknowledge a bad choice helps us assess him. Competence requires more evidence than candor. If he repeatedly identifies the right problem while the same failure persists, confidence in his ability to repair it should decline even if confidence in his honesty remains intact.

A difficult stretch would need to be judged against the team available, the resources committed and the decisions made by both football leaders. Injuries may change what is reasonable to expect. A weak acquisition may expose an error in Caldwell's evaluation. A workable roster may still be poorly taught or used. Our own original assumptions belong in that review. We selected the leadership arrangement and approved the commitment; we cannot fairly treat our initial assessment as beyond question while examining everyone else's.

Any eventual case for a change would also need a credible account of what comes next. A replacement brings his own staff, expectations of authority, quarterback preferences and roster demands. We would have to compare that undertaking with what the current staff can reasonably accomplish. Continuing with Stone now leaves a separate question about a longer commitment. An extension would ask us to project beyond the existing contract. One completed season and a good September give us less evidence for that judgment than for keeping the present operation together.

The next meaningful review should return to the unfinished work: whether Cousins's protection and decision work holds up in games, whether the defense can finish possessions, whether individual development survives the weekly schedule and whether Stone can carry his enlarged calling role without losing oversight elsewhere. Protection and ball security were already part of Caldwell's January assessment. They belong in the next review alongside the defensive third downs and the effect of calling both units.
