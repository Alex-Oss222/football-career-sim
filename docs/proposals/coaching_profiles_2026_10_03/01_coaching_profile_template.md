# Alex Stone: Coaching Profile

*Proposed revision for review.*

Alex Stone enjoys an argument about football. He asks which calls his quarterback trusts, why a receiver thinks a route will work and what an assistant sees that he has missed. He will spend time on an equipment staff member's idea or a fan's drawing, working through the football with the person who brought it to him. If he leaves it out, he explains why.

His meetings are relaxed and often funny. Players can sound like themselves. A quiet player does not have to become a talker, and an outgoing one does not have to sit through the week pretending he has no sense of humor. Stone recognizes birthdays, new babies and family milestones. He can tell a veteran in front of the position group that his technique is poor and have him and his family over for dinner that evening.

Practice moves quickly. His corrections are short, technical and direct. He notices the back who recognizes pressure, the receiver who keeps the proper depth and the guard who stays on a combination long enough for the other blocker to leave. He is quick to praise those plays. The star and the reserve get the same scrutiny when the film comes on.

## Preparing the week

Stone starts with the opponent's players. A linebacker struggling to cover a back interests him. So does a corner who gives up the inside release, or a defensive tackle whose eagerness to penetrate creates room behind him. He looks for the formation, personnel and situation that will let him reach that player. He is willing to keep returning to a productive call until the defense changes its answer.

The staff prepares cut-ups and written reports on the team and its individual players. Stone wants the relevant clips available when a call is proposed. The conversation has to reach the protection, the likely defensive adjustment and the time needed to practice it. If the new idea requires unfamiliar work, something else comes off the script.

His favorite designs grow out of familiar football. Outside zone, a play-action crossing route and a bootleg can begin with much the same picture. He watches how the edge defender and safety respond, then chooses the next call with that response in mind. When the defense begins anticipating a formation's usual action, he looks for a shot or a prepared trick play.

He also watches what his own offense is announcing. If two tight ends have come to mean a run, he looks for a pass worth calling from that personnel. Motion can expose how the defense is accounting for a receiver, change the strength or improve a release.

The quarterback's and players' accounts belong in this preparation. Stone asks which routes they trust, which blocks feel natural and where they hesitate. Their answers can change the first assignment, the help around it or how he teaches it. He expects them to know enough of the offense to contribute to the discussion.

## The offense

Stone calls his own offense. He favors multiple tight ends, play-action, backs who can do several jobs and a passing game that gives the quarterback ways to work the middle of the field. His weekly emphasis follows the available players and the defense in front of them. A preferred run-pass split will not pull him away from a back the opponent cannot cover or a run it has not stopped.

He is particularly comfortable in 12 and 13 personnel. Two or three tight ends give him different blocking surfaces, and he can move them among attached, wing and detached positions. A receiving tight end can work from the slot or wing while his attached blocking develops. Another can take a pulling assignment or supply help on the edge. Stone wants useful differences among those players without every substitution telling the defense exactly what is coming.

No-huddle helps him retain the personnel matchup and control the snap. Some series move quickly. On others, the offense gets aligned early and the quarterback has time to inspect the defense. Stone will huddle to substitute or deliver a more involved call.

Dagger is one of his preferred ways to attack the intermediate middle. The inside vertical threatens the safety, the dig enters the space underneath and a shallow route occupies the underneath coverage. He wants the quarterback ready to take the vertical when it is available. If the linebackers are stepping toward the run, play-action can open room for the dig. Post-dig and post-cross combinations let him work a safety at different depths, particularly when that safety has begun driving downhill.

Against man coverage, he likes Mesh. The shallow crossers need spacing and timing that allow them to run through traffic without blocking defenders. He spends practice time on those details. Against zone, a sit route or taught settle adjustment gives the quarterback a different answer. A back releasing on a wheel can provide a deeper matchup to examine before working the crossing routes. Stone wants the receivers and quarterback to recognize the same adjustment.

His outside-zone action carries into Boot Flood, with a deep route, an intermediate route and a flat release on the rollout side. Before moving the quarterback, he pays particular attention to the backside edge defender.

Slant-flat gives him a quick throw to one side. Screens are a substantial part of his offense: a slow screen to a back against an eager rush, a tunnel screen bringing a receiver toward his blockers, a quick perimeter throw into space or a tight-end screen after an initial block. The versions require different releases and timing. He wants the actual one on the sheet practiced by the players who will run it.

Protection stays in the conversation when he selects the pass. He will keep extra help in for a shot he likes, shorten the throw when the line is losing a matchup or change the launch point. An overmatched tackle can receive a chip. The quarterback needs to know which pressures the protection handles and where the ball goes against an extra rusher. If the throw cannot be reached before the rush arrives, Stone changes the timing or protection.

The running game carries both zone and gap concepts. Inside zone gives him combinations into the linebackers and room for the runner to find a crease. Outside zone stretches the front laterally. He wants the back to press the aiming point long enough to influence pursuit, then make the coached read from the blocks as they develop. He narrows the weekly work around the line and backs he has.

Power gives him down blocks, double teams and a pulling guard leading toward a linebacker. Counter changes the initial backfield action and brings pullers from the other side. He can use a guard and tackle or put the wrapping job on an H-back. He watches whether the lead blocker reaches the linebacker cleanly and whether the runner stays with that block long enough. Trap appeals to him against an interior defender coming upfield. Jet sweep is a real outside handoff, with the same motion accompanying inside runs and play-action.

He likes a committee of backs with overlapping skills. He can feature the outside-zone runner, use another behind pullers and lean on his strongest protector in passing situations. A powerful runner can begin with early-down work while learning protection. Stone changes the help and the assignment before giving up on the player. He will also remove a favored package when the players available cannot carry it, keeping the terminology and parts of the offense they can use.

The quarterback has authority to change Stone's call within the offense he knows. A younger quarterback may begin with a smaller menu and add checks as he learns. Changing the call does not itself bring punishment. Stone wants to examine the read and the decision afterward, including a successful play that came from a poor decision. He teaches the quarterback to connect his feet to the progression and asks which throws he is seeing clearly.

## The defense

Stone's offensive background shows in the questions he asks about defense. He wants to know the protection rule being attacked, the blocker being isolated and the throw the opposing quarterback trusts when pressure arrives. He stays involved in the plan when a coordinator is calling it. He wants the front and coverage connected, with enough disguise to make the quarterback confirm the picture after the snap.

His preference is pressure with four rushers and seven in coverage. Tackle-end games and alignment changes can make the line pass rushers off. Interior push takes away the quarterback's room to step up. Against a mobile quarterback, Stone watches the escape lane as well as the first pressure.

He likes man coverage with help placed where the offense wants to throw. Cover 1 Robber appeals to him against digs and crossers: one safety stays deep, an underneath defender works the middle and the man defenders use leverage to direct receivers toward that help. Against bunches and stacks, he wants the exchange settled before the offense can use traffic to free somebody.

Cover 3 gives him a zone changeup from a similar single-high picture. He also likes showing two safeties high and rotating late. He asks the underneath defenders which receivers they are carrying and which they are passing off, especially when a seam is being sent behind a crossing route.

He will show six or seven players near the line and send four, change the side supplying the rush or drop a capable lineman into the expected quick-throw lane. When he adds a fifth rusher, the discussion includes the coverage behind it and the throw the defense is being asked to concede or contest.

In the run game, he wants the front controlling its gaps and keeping the linebackers able to fit. He likes slants and coordinated exchanges that change blocking angles, with the linebacker fitting behind the movement. He judges penetration by the runner's path. A defender arriving in the backfield while the ball escapes through his gap still gets corrected.

Against zone, Stone follows the combination block and the backside cutback. He wants the line making the climb to the linebacker difficult, the linebackers staying connected to the runner's track and the backside pursuit closing without overrunning it. The force defender keeps the outside boundary intact. Against Power and Counter, he follows the puller through the fit. If the edge spills the block, support has to be outside it. If the call is to box, the edge keeps outside leverage and turns the runner toward the inside help.

A shifted tight end can change the gaps and the force assignment. The supporting safety, corner or nickel needs to know which answer the front is playing. Against zone read, Stone wants the handoff and quarterback responsibilities named, including an exchange between the end and linebacker. Adding a moving blocker cannot leave those jobs implicit.

## Teaching and practice

Stone wants a player to understand how his assignment affects somebody else's. The receiver's route changes another receiver's space. The back's protection allows someone to release. A defender's alignment and key lead into the fit or coverage responsibility. He wants that understanding available when the opponent changes the picture.

He distinguishes an assignment the player never understood from poor technique, a bad decision or a lack of effort. Each calls for a different correction. Coaches have to answer for conflicting instructions and calls their players cannot execute. He is patient with someone admitting confusion. He has little patience for someone agreeing with everything and arriving unprepared again.

Vague explanations hold up the meeting. If the communication was bad, Stone wants to know who made the call, what the other player heard and which assignment followed. On film, the staff freezes the picture before the snap. The player identifies the front or coverage clue and states the assignment. The coach runs the play, locates the mistake and returns to the point where the answer changed. Correct and incorrect examples lead into a walkthrough and then a practice rep.

Stone likes a hard camp: sustained work, quick movement between periods and enough repetition to expose conditioning and communication problems. He remains approachable on the field. Players and assistants can ask questions.

Individual work leads into group periods and team work. Receivers work releases, depths and coverage adjustments; backs work their run track, protection and receiving jobs; linemen repeat footwork and exchanges. Inside run gets attention. So do passing-game timing and the connections between the defensive front and coverage. Seven-on-seven has a place in the week, while protection is examined with a rush present.

On a failed combination block, the coach resets the front and identifies the linebacker. The linemen walk through the exchange. They repeat it at the period's tempo. The responsibility returns in team work. Later, the staff checks it without giving the instruction again first.

Stone uses sustained drives, sometimes ten or twelve snaps, to see whether calls and alignments hold as players tire. Backed-up, red-zone, two-minute and four-minute periods receive their own time and the urgency of the situation. He varies personnel groups so starters and reserves get meaningful work with different teammates and opponents. Reserves receive individual feedback, and increased responsibility follows work the staff can identify.

Players and coaches, including Stone, have written physical standards appropriate to their duties and health. A failed test brings an individual plan and a retest. Medical restrictions and applicable practice limits govern the work. When a football correction keeps failing, Stone and the position coach change the setting or assignment. He has little interest in repeating the same instruction louder.

## During the game

Stone prepares an opening menu of roughly thirty calls. Familiar formations and concepts help him find out how the opponent intends to defend him. Short yardage or a backed-up possession takes him to that part of the sheet. Unused opening calls remain available.

On the sideline, he stays relaxed and keeps the conversation moving. A frustrated quarterback gets a hearing. Stone asks what changed, which throws he is seeing and which calls he trusts. He talks through the last possession with the quarterback and staff before settling on the next answer.

A defender sitting in the dig window may leave the shallow answer available or call for another prepared concept. A late throw needs to be separated from a protection failure. A rounded break goes back to the position coach. His larger adjustments come from practiced football: quick game, screens, added protection or a different running emphasis. Changes to routes use shared tags and landmarks so the quarterback and receiver leave with the same call.

With the ball and a lead, he keeps looking for points. He wants first downs that retain possession and another score that makes a comeback harder. A prepared pass remains part of his four-minute offense. Before the possession, he talks with the quarterback about the acceptable outcome: the covered throw, sack risk, opponent's timeouts and when to snap. As the situation changes, he can place more weight on keeping the clock moving and making the defense spend a timeout.

His defensive instinct with a fourth-quarter lead is more cautious. He prefers completions in front, protection against the deep ball and tackles inbounds. With a narrow lead, that preference can concede short gains toward field-goal range. In a tie, he is more inclined to pressure the quarterback and seek a disruptive play.

Two-minute work has a practiced procedure for the next call, inbounds completions and stopping the clock. An assistant keeps timeout, fourth-down and challenge information reaching Stone before the decision. Stone makes the call.

The kicking game gets meeting time, practice periods and weight in the active roster. A reserve's coverage or protection work can earn him a game-day place. Stone reviews protection errors, coverage lanes, return decisions and penalties with the attention he gives offense. The coordinator owns the technical plan; Stone stays involved in field-goal, punt, fourth-down and fake decisions. He wants the choice communicated before units begin coming on and off the field. He enjoys a well-designed fake and wants to see the opponent's look and his players' execution in preparation before authorizing it.

## His staff and the front office

Stone hires assistants who know their position and can teach it. He values the coach who identifies a protection problem, explains its cause and brings a correction. He expects an honest account of what a group can do and where its development has stalled. An assistant can challenge the plan, advocate for a player or tell him the install is too large.

Position coaches choose their drills and examples. Changes to shared assignments go through the other coaches affected. Stone settles disagreements before players receive competing instructions. He wants coordinators with enough responsibility to run their units and enough confidence to disagree with him.

He tells assistants he will let them finish their corrections. He cuts into an offensive correction before the position coach has finished.

He is comfortable working with a general manager who holds final personnel authority. His requests describe the football job: the edge blocks, combinations and routes an attached tight end needs to carry, what the staff needs immediately, what it can teach and where help will be necessary. A role player can come through the draft, a low-cost signing or another team's cuts. He does not need an expensive name to see a useful addition.

His belief in development can make him more optimistic about a prospect than the personnel staff. He sees a role and a way to teach the missing part. The general manager can disagree about the time or roster place that work deserves. Stone argues his priorities and coaches the roster the general manager chooses.

## Publicly and privately

Stone is measured, articulate and composed in public. He uses humor when it fits and speaks plainly when the team has played badly. His first response to criticism is to take responsibility for the call, the preparation or the situation he put his players in. He will blame his call when he knows a player missed the assignment. The individual correction stays inside the building.

He gives specific credit, including after losses. A reserve's preparation, an assistant's adjustment or a good block can be worth naming. After a poor defensive performance, he can discuss missed tackles and the work ahead without making the press conference an accusation against one player. The actual mistake is examined in the meeting afterward. Repeated carelessness brings a private disciplinary conversation and can affect playing time.

His relationships with players continue through injuries, poor stretches and reduced roles. He spends time with their families and remains involved when the football is going badly. A player can keep parts of his life private. Stone wants medical help reachable at any hour and access to multiple qualified psychologists, and encourages players to use that support. Clinicians control clearance, treatment and restrictions.

He wants a family liaison whom spouses and partners can approach about problems at home, including abuse by a player. Safety reports go to qualified help and the appropriate reporting process. He will not suppress a report to protect an important player or treat it as something he can settle over dinner.

A player he knows well can still lose snaps or a roster place. Stone explains the football decision directly. Meals and contact with the family can continue, and the relationship can remain strained.

## Current Jacksonville application

Through September 28, 2014: Stone calls the offense; Mike Tice coordinates its weekly work. Romeo Crennel called the defense through Week 3. Stone's September 21 instruction transferred defensive calling to him from Week 4, with Crennel continuing to coordinate preparation and transmit calls. Mike Westhoff runs special teams. Dave Caldwell retains final acquisition, contract and cap authority. The Week 4 backfield is an instructed hierarchy of Maurice Jones-Drew, C.J. Anderson and Jonathan Grimes. These are dated assignments and decisions alongside Stone's broader preferences.
