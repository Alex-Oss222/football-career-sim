## 1. The coach in the building

[Open with three or four paragraphs that a player or assistant could recognize. Describe his conversational manner, appetite for football, expectations and response to disagreement. Show what the relaxed atmosphere permits and what he still insists upon. Avoid a paragraph of leadership adjectives.]

### Stone sample

Alex Stone enjoys an argument about football. He asks the quarterback which throws he trusts and listens when a receiver questions a route. An assistant can tell him the install is too large. He wants the relevant clips on the screen and an explanation of the protection, the adjustment and the practice time the idea needs.

His meetings are relaxed and often funny. Quiet players can stay quiet; outgoing players do not have to stop being themselves. He notices birthdays, new babies and family milestones. He can correct a veteran's technique in front of his position group and have him and his family over for dinner that evening.

That familiarity does not remove the work. Stone wants to know who made the call, what the other player heard and which assignment followed. Admitting confusion gives him something to teach. Agreeing with everything and returning unprepared does not.

[If later requested, describe his humor through an approved example or recorded exchange. Do not manufacture catchphrases, profanity, practical jokes, private rituals or speeches to make him seem distinctive.]

## 2. What he notices when he studies football

[Describe the order of his attention. Does a personnel weakness lead him toward a concept, or does a favored concept send him looking for a suitable opponent? Show how he tests an appealing idea and what makes him leave it out. Explain self-scouting without inventing a proprietary scouting system.]

### Stone sample

Stone starts with the opposing players. He looks for a linebacker who struggles with a back in coverage, a corner vulnerable to an inside release or an interior defender whose penetration opens a running lane. He then looks for the formation and situation that let him reach that matchup.

He gives an idea a hearing regardless of whose name is attached to it. An assistant, a player, an equipment staff member or a fan's drawing can start the discussion. Keeping the idea requires more: it has to fit the protection and the players' preparation. Unfamiliar work takes time from something else on the script.

## 3. Offensive identity and personnel

[Explain the offense's recurring relationships: personnel, formations, motion, tempo and the matchups he wants. Identify what he prefers across jobs and what depends on a particular roster. A reader should understand why his familiar calls belong together.]

### Stone sample

Stone follows the matchup. If the defense cannot cover a back or stop a run, he is willing to keep returning to it. A target run-pass split carries less weight than the advantage in front of him. He watches the response and prepares the call that attacks it.

He favors 12 and 13 personnel and moves tight ends among attached, wing and detached alignments. Motion can change strength, improve a release or show how the defense is handling a matchup. No-huddle lets him keep a personnel group on the field; it does not always mean an immediate snap. He can align early and let the quarterback examine the defense, then huddle when substitutions or a more involved call require it.

## 4. Passing game, quarterback and protection

### How he tries to create a throw

[Connect the core passing families to the defender or coverage relationship they attack. Include answers against man, zone, pressure and changing leverage. Describe Stone's reasons for choosing them; route diagrams, exact landmarks, tags and progressions remain in the active playbook.]

### Stone sample

Dagger appeals to Stone because it gives him access to the intermediate middle while the inside vertical threatens the safety. He still wants the quarterback ready to take that vertical when it is available. Mesh gives him a familiar answer against man coverage, provided the crossers' timing and spacing let them run through traffic without blocking defenders. Against zone, a taught settle adjustment or a sit route changes that answer.

Post-dig and post-cross combinations work a safety at different depths. Boot-action Flood belongs with outside zone, but the backside edge defender matters to the decision to move the quarterback. His screens have distinct jobs: draw an eager rush toward a back screen, bring a receiver toward blockers on a tunnel screen or release a tight end after an initial block. The staff practices the actual timing of the version he intends to call.

### What he asks of the quarterback

[Cover preparation, permitted checks, footwork and progression teaching, how he listens on the sideline, and how he evaluates a decision separately from its outcome. Do not assign a preferred quarterback personality or physical type unless adopted.]

Stone gives the quarterback authority to change a call within the offense he knows. A younger quarterback can begin with a smaller menu and gain checks as he learns. Stone reviews the read afterward, including successful plays that came from a poor decision. A frustrated quarterback gets a hearing on the sideline: which throws is he seeing, what changed, and which calls does he trust for the next possession?

### How protection changes the call

[Describe the relationship between protection, release count, timing and available answers. Explain how he distinguishes a recognition error, a physical loss, a late throw and an uncovered extra rusher.]

Stone will keep help in for a shot he likes, move the launch point or shorten the throw when the line is losing a matchup. The quarterback needs to know which pressures the protection handles and where the ball goes against an extra rusher. Adding help affects which receivers can release. The profile should name that choice without recreating the protection manual.

## 5. Running game and the backs

[Explain why he carries both zone and gap football, how the line and backs narrow the weekly work, and how he judges an unsuccessful run. Connect formations and complementary passes to the run family.]

### Stone sample

Stone carries inside and outside zone alongside Power, Counter and Trap. He narrows the work around his blockers and backs. On zone, he wants the runner to press the aiming point long enough to influence pursuit and make the coached read as the blocks develop. On Power and Counter, he watches whether the lead blocker reaches the linebacker and whether the runner stays with that block long enough.

Trap interests him against an interior defender getting upfield. Jet sweep is a real handoff, with the same motion used alongside inside runs and play-action. He watches whether the defense widens, exchanges responsibilities or continues chasing the initial action.

He likes backs with overlapping skills and a committee as a general preference. Jacksonville's instruction from Week 4 of 2014 instead establishes Jones-Drew first, Anderson second and Grimes third. That dated decision belongs beside the preference, with its controlling source. It does not erase the preference or justify treating every later backfield as a committee.

[When evidence supports it, explain how protection, receiving, workload and the week's run assignments affect usage within the approved order. Do not invent snap shares or a rotation policy.]

## 6. Defensive identity and pass defense

[Describe Stone's defensive interests separately from who calls the unit. Explain how the front, rush and coverage support one another. Calling authority may change while his football preferences remain recognizable.]

### Stone sample

Stone prefers to create pressure with four and retain seven in coverage. Alignment changes and tackle-end games can force the protection to pass rushers off. Against a mobile quarterback, an escape lane matters along with the first pressure. He values interior push because it takes away the space to step up.

He likes man coverage with help where the opponent wants to throw. Cover 1 Robber gives him an underneath defender working the middle against digs and crossers, with leverage directing receivers toward that help. Cover 3 supplies a zone changeup from a similar single-high picture. He also likes showing two safeties high and rotating late.

Bunches and stacks require a settled exchange before the snap. Underneath defenders must know what they carry and what they pass off. Showing six or seven near the line and sending four can make a protection declare without automatically reducing the number in coverage. When he sends a fifth, the pressure discussion includes the coverage behind it and the throw the defense is accepting or contesting.

[Develop the cost through the actual assignment: disguise must still permit sound alignment, and added pressure changes the coverage burden. Do not assign a blitz percentage, preferred base front or permanent man-coverage identity merely because he likes particular answers.]

## 7. Run defense, tackling and pursuit

[Cover gap control, combinations, force and cutback responsibilities, gap-scheme fits, motion and quarterback runs. Describe what he asks the staff to diagnose rather than listing fronts.]

### Stone sample

Stone judges penetration by what it does to the runner's path. A defender getting into the backfield while the ball escapes through his assigned gap still needs correcting. Slants and exchanges must connect to the linebacker fitting behind them.

Against zone, he follows the combination block, the climb to the linebacker and the backside cutback. Against Power and Counter, he follows the puller through the fit. A spill call needs support outside the edge; a box call needs the edge to keep outside leverage and turn the runner toward inside help. A shifted tight end can change the gaps and force assignment. Against zone read, the handoff and quarterback responsibilities must be named.

## 8. Special teams as part of the team

[Address kicking and punt protection, coverage, returns, specialists, roster places and possession-changing decisions. Explain the boundary between the coordinator's technical work and Stone's decisions. Do not pretend his favorite fake is a full special-teams philosophy.]

### Stone sample

Stone gives special teams meeting time and practice periods, and coverage or protection work can earn a reserve a game-day place. The coordinator owns the technical plan. Stone remains involved in field goal, punt, fourth-down and fake decisions, and wants the choice communicated before units begin moving on and off the field.

He reviews protection errors, coverage lanes, return decisions and penalties with the same attention he gives offense. He enjoys a prepared fake when the opponent's look and his players' execution support it.

## 9. Calling the game and handling its situations

[Explain opening information-gathering, between-series adjustments and priorities when the score or clock changes. Cover third down, short yardage, red zone, backed-up possessions, two-minute and four-minute work through established preferences. Leave unestablished fourth-down or two-point policies open.]

### Stone sample

Stone prepares an opening menu of roughly thirty calls. A backed-up possession or short-yardage need takes him to that part of the sheet; the opening menu is not a sequence he must finish. Between possessions, he distinguishes a covered route from a late throw or protection failure. His larger changes come from practiced answers: quick game, screens, a different run emphasis or added protection.

With a lead, he still looks for first downs and points. A prepared pass remains available in four-minute offense. He talks with the quarterback about the covered throw, sack risk, timeouts and snap timing. As the situation changes, keeping the clock moving can become more important.

His defensive instinct with a fourth-quarter lead is more cautious. He wants completions in front and tackles inbounds. A narrow lead can make the conceded short gains costly as the opponent approaches field-goal range. In a tie, he is more inclined to pressure the quarterback. Preserve that difference rather than describing him as uniformly aggressive or conservative.

## 10. Teaching, practice and training camp

[Describe the day's football work, the correction sequence and what he checks later. Explain the demands of camp, participation standards and meaningful repetitions without inventing a completed workout or a universal daily schedule.]

### Stone sample

Stone likes a hard camp with sustained work, quick transitions and enough repetition to expose conditioning and communication problems. He stays approachable. Questions can be asked, and corrections need to be short enough to use on the next rep.

The coach resets the front. The linemen identify the linebacker. They walk through the combination and exchange, repeat it at the period's tempo and take the responsibility back into team work. Later, the staff returns to it without repeating the instruction first.

Individual work feeds group periods and team work. Seven-on-seven has its place; protection is examined with a rush present. Sustained drives can test whether alignments and calls hold as players tire. Backed-up, red-zone, two-minute and four-minute periods need the urgency of their situations.

Stars and reserves work to the same standard. When the correction keeps failing, Stone and the position coach change the teaching setting or assignment. Players and coaches, including Stone, have written physical standards appropriate to their duties and health. A failed test leads to an individual plan and retest. Medical restrictions and applicable practice limits remain in force.

## 11. Development, roles and disappointing a player

[Explain how he finds an immediate role, teaches its connections and decides whether responsibility can grow. Include reserves, reduced roles and stalled development. Avoid turning his belief in teaching into guaranteed player improvement.]

### Stone sample

A receiving tight end can begin in the slot or wing while attached blocking develops. A powerful runner can contribute on early downs while learning protection. Stone changes the help and assignment before giving up on a player, and removes a favored package when the available players cannot carry it.

Film work connects the assignment to the picture. Freeze before the snap, identify the clue, have the player state the job, then locate where the answer changed. Correct and incorrect examples lead to a walkthrough and a practice rep. The explanation has to become something the player can execute.

More responsibility follows identifiable work. A reduced role gets a direct explanation. His contact with the player and family continues, but the relationship can remain strained.

## 12. Assistants and front-office relationships

[Give the assistants meaningful authority and room to disagree. Explain which disagreements require a common decision, how Stone intervenes and how personnel requests reach the GM. Keep current names and titles in the dated application note.]

### Stone sample

Stone hires assistants who know their position and can teach it. Position coaches choose their drills and examples. Changes to shared assignments go through the other affected coaches, so players do not receive competing instructions. Assistants can challenge a plan or advocate for a player.

He tells assistants he will let them finish their corrections. He cuts into an offensive correction before the position coach has finished.

His personnel requests describe jobs: the edge blocks, combinations and routes an attached tight end must perform, what is needed immediately, what the staff can teach and where help is necessary. Caldwell retains final authority over acquisitions, contracts and the cap. Stone argues his evaluation and coaches the roster that results.

His belief in development can make him more optimistic about a prospect than the personnel staff. Seeing a useful role does not settle how much time or a roster place that project deserves. A disagreement can remain a disagreement after Caldwell's decision.

## 13. Public conduct, private correction and family relationships

[Preserve the range of his interpersonal behavior. Public composure, humor, direct correction and genuine interest in families should not be compressed into a single culture slogan. Do not invent Stone's own domestic arrangements.]

### Stone sample

Stone is measured and articulate in public, with humor when it fits. He takes responsibility for calls and preparation and gives specific credit to a good block, a reserve's preparation or an assistant's adjustment. He will blame his call when he knows a player missed an assignment. Inside the building, the actual mistake still gets examined.

Meals and conversations continue through injuries and poor stretches. A player can keep parts of his life private. Stone wants medical help reachable at any hour, access to multiple qualified psychologists and a family liaison spouses and partners can approach about problems at home. Clinicians control treatment and clearance. Safety reports go to qualified help and the appropriate reporting process.

A close relationship does not guarantee snaps or a roster place. He explains the football decision and continues the relationship without assuming the player will welcome either conversation.
