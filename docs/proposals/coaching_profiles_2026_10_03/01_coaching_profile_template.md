# Alex Stone: Coaching Profile

*Proposed revision for review.*

Alex Stone enjoys an argument about football. He asks which calls his quarterback trusts, why a receiver thinks a route will work and what an assistant sees that he has missed. He will spend time on an equipment staff member's idea or a fan's drawing, working through the football with the person who brought it to him. If he leaves it out, he explains why.

His meetings are relaxed and often funny. Quiet players can stay quiet; outgoing players can be funny. Stone recognizes birthdays, new babies and family milestones. He can tell a veteran in front of the position group that his technique is poor and have him and his family over for dinner that evening.

Practice moves quickly. His corrections are short, technical and direct. He notices the back who recognizes pressure, the receiver who keeps the proper depth and the guard who stays on a combination long enough for the other blocker to leave. He is quick to praise those plays. The star and the reserve get the same scrutiny when the film comes on.

## Preparing for the week

Stone has his staff prepare cut-ups and written reports on the opponent's team and individual players. He studies the last several games for their tendencies, their style of play and what they do well. He wants the clips behind the report: the coverages and pressures they keep returning to, the plays that have hurt them, and the adjustments they made when an offense went back to those plays.

Against a defense, he studies the coordinator along with the players. What does he call on early downs? What changes on third-and-short, third-and-long or in the red zone? Which fronts and coverages does he use against two tight ends, three receivers or an empty backfield? Stone checks how the defense adjusts to motion, bunches and shifts, which pressures come from those adjustments, and whether the safety shows the rotation before the snap. He compares the recent games to see whether a call is a regular part of the defense or something they used against a particular opponent.

The individual reports get into technique. With an edge rusher, Stone looks at his get-off, his first move, how he uses his hands and what he comes back with when the tackle stops it. With an interior lineman, he looks at whether he plays through the blocker or tries to beat him into a gap, and how he handles a double team. A corner's leverage, jam, feet at the line and turn out of his backpedal can affect which release and route Stone wants to use against him. He studies how linebackers react to the backfield, carry a tight end or pick up a back, and how quickly the safeties commit to the run.

Those details go into the plan. An edge rusher who keeps getting upfield may give him a screen or a run underneath. A linebacker who struggles to turn with a back may get an angle route or a wheel. If a safety is driving on the dig, Stone looks at the post behind him. He also wants the staff to show what happened when another team tried the same thing. The opponent may already have changed its coverage, added help or altered the defender's assignment.

He brings the quarterback and position groups into that work. Stone asks which throws the quarterback is seeing clearly, which releases the receivers like against those corners and where the line expects to need help. A proposed pass comes with a protection. A new formation or motion has to be practiced with the people who will run it. Third-down, red-zone, backed-up and two-minute calls get their own work during the week.

He checks his own tendencies too. If the second tight end has been bringing a run into the game, he wants a pass from the same group. If the defense has seen Power from an alignment, Counter or Power Pass can come from it as well. By the end of the week, he cuts calls that have looked poor in practice or ask a player to do something he cannot yet execute. The final sheet keeps the calls the team is ready to use against that opponent.

## Offense

Stone calls his own offense. He is comfortable in 12 and 13 personnel, but he will use one tight end, two, three or none, spread the field with four receivers, put two backs together or bring in another lineman. The players available and the matchup decide what he uses. If the defense cannot stop a run or cover a particular receiver, he will keep going after it. He has no interest in calling a pass just to even up the run-pass count, or changing a productive call because he has already used it.

### Personnel and formations

His offense carries 10, 11, 12, 13, 20, 21 and 22 personnel, along with a six-offensive-lineman package. In 11, he has one back, one tight end and three receivers, with much of the running and passing game available. In 10, the fourth receiver replaces the tight end. He uses that group to spread the defense, identify pressure, work the quick passing game and run draws or selected inside runs against the lighter box.

Twelve personnel gives him one back, two tight ends and two receivers. He can attach both tight ends, put one at wing or move them out into receiving positions. He likes being able to run from a heavier formation, then spread the same players and see which defenders go with the tight ends. Thirteen adds a third tight end. He can build another blocking surface, run Power, Counter or Duo, then come back with a boot, crossing route, tight-end screen or a pass with extra protection. Bringing in more tight ends does not take the passing game off the sheet.

He uses two-back personnel in different ways. Twenty puts two backs with three receivers and no tight end. One back can stay beside the quarterback while the other moves out for a route, giving him the angle, wheel and screen game against linebackers. Twenty-one adds a tight end with two receivers; he can use a fullback to lead, protect or release, work from under center and carry his play-action passes. Twenty-two gives him two backs and two tight ends for short yardage, the goal line and four-minute football, with a play-action pass available from the same formation.

The extra-lineman package has runs toward the added blocker and away from him, along with a protected shot. Stone wants the defense to account for the extra size without being able to dismiss the remaining receivers. He does not force a grouping onto a roster that cannot run it. Two useful receiving backs can give him more from 20 personnel than an unsuitable player pressed into a fullback job.

Formation and personnel are separate decisions. Two tight ends can line up in a spread set, and a back can move out to make an empty formation without being replaced by a receiver. Stone uses Doubles, Trips, Trey, Bunch, Ace, Wing, Pro, I, Empty and heavier or unbalanced sets from the groupings suited to them. A tight end may be attached on one call and in the slot on the next. A receiver can move into a stack or bunch to help his release. The underlying route concept stays familiar while those alignments change who has to cover it.

### No-huddle and tempo

No-huddle is Stone's preferred way to operate. He can get the offense lined up early, let the quarterback study the front and coverage, and still change the formation or send a player in motion. When he likes the matchup, he keeps the same eleven on the field and can increase the pace. He does not need to snap immediately on every no-huddle play.

He also uses substitutions to find the matchup. He may bring in two tight ends, see the defense answer with bigger personnel, then spread the tight ends without making another substitution. His playbook calls that HOLD. PRESS keeps those players on and speeds up the offense with a smaller menu of familiar calls. When he changes personnel again, he allows for the defense's chance to match before deciding whether to stay with that group.

The no-huddle calls use the same short formation and concept language the players already know, delivered verbally, through a signal and word, or through the week's wristband. Stone will huddle when he wants to substitute, communicate a more involved call or manage the clock. The quarterback still needs time to identify pressure and make the required check.

### Passing game

Stone likes working the middle of the field with Dagger, Drive, Mesh and Y-Cross. He can move those concepts among compatible personnel groups and formations without teaching a different offense each time. A tight end may run a job that belonged to a receiver in another grouping, or a back may release into the route from the backfield instead of lining up wide. He chooses the player and alignment that give him the matchup he wants.

Dagger is one of his preferred intermediate throws. The inside vertical takes a defender deep and the dig breaks into the space underneath. Stone wants the quarterback to recognize when the seam itself is open, then get to the dig if the coverage carries the vertical. Drive puts a shallow cross underneath a deeper dig, making the linebackers handle routes at different depths. Y-Cross lets him send a tight end or H-back across behind the linebackers, with a vertical route clearing space above him. Play-action can help those throws when the second level is reacting hard to the run.

Against man coverage, he likes Mesh, especially from Bunch or another alignment that makes the defenders work through traffic. The crossers have to keep their spacing and avoid turning the play into an illegal pick. Against zone, the sit route and the taught settle adjustments give the quarterback somewhere to throw when defenders pass the crossers off. He can add a wheel when he likes the back's matchup. Texas gives him another way to use that back, releasing outside before breaking back in on the angle. Choice routes are available for a trusted receiver or tight end who reads leverage the same way as the quarterback.

Stick, Spacing and Slant-Flat give him quick completions against cushion or pressure. On Slant-Flat, he watches whether the underneath defender widens with the flat route or stays inside under the slant. Smash puts a hitch or flat underneath a corner route; Curl-Flat lets him work the sideline against a defender caught between the two depths. He will take those short throws repeatedly if the defense keeps leaving them available.

He wants deeper throws in the offense as well. Four Verticals stretches the deep coverage and lets him work the seams or an isolated outside receiver. Mills pairs the dig with a post behind it, useful against a safety coming downhill on the dig. Post-Cross gives him a deep post and a crossing route behind the linebackers, often off hard run action with extra protection. Stone looks for the safety or corner whose reaction makes the shot worth calling. If that defender stays deep, the quarterback still has to come off the throw.

The play-action game uses runs the defense has actually seen. Power Pass follows the pull and downhill action of Power. Lead Post comes off the two-back lead game. Outside Zone sets up Boot Flood, with routes at three levels on the rollout side; Counter Boot moves the quarterback away from the counter action. Stone checks how the backside end is playing before calling a naked boot. A defender waiting outside for the quarterback can take away the time needed for the crossing route.

Screens are a regular part of his passing game. He likes the slow screen to a back against a rush getting upfield, the quicker slip screen against pressure, and tunnel screens from reduced receiver splits. Smoke and bubble throws let him use space outside when the numbers and leverage permit it. A tight end can show a block before releasing on a delay screen. The linemen's release, the back's timing and the receiver's blocking job have to fit the particular screen; he practices the version he intends to call.

Stone pairs the pass with its protection. He will keep a back or tight end in for a deeper throw, chip an overmatched tackle's man, shorten the route or move the launch point. Before the game, the quarterback needs to know which pressures the protection handles and where the ball goes against an extra rusher. A pass he likes can come off the sheet if the line cannot give it enough time.

The quarterback can change Stone's call within the checks he has been taught. A younger starter begins with fewer choices and earns more as he learns the offense. Stone reviews what the quarterback saw, his footwork and when he released the ball. A completed pass does not excuse a poor read, and an audible does not bring punishment simply because it changed the coach's call.

### Running game

Stone likes a physical running game, with Power and Counter alongside Inside and Outside Zone. He uses under-center and shotgun runs, double teams, pulling linemen, fullbacks and H-backs. Spreading the receivers does not mean he has given up running downhill. He selects the weekly runs around his blockers, his backs and the way the opponent fits the run.

On Power, he wants the down blocks secured, movement from the double team and the puller arriving where the back can follow him. Counter lets him use the same downhill blocking with a change in the back's initial direction. He can pull the guard and tackle or have an H-back wrap behind the guard. He likes it when linebackers are flowing hard toward the first movement or overplaying Power. Duo gives him vertical double teams with the back reading the linebacker; Lead or Iso puts a lead blocker directly onto a second-level defender.

Inside Zone lets the line work combinations against different fronts, with the back pressing his inside track before making the cut. Outside Zone stretches the front and makes the defenders run laterally. Stone wants the back to stay on his track long enough for the blocks and pursuit to develop, then plant and go. On Split Zone, the tight end or H-back crosses behind the line to handle the backside edge. The related boot or leak can come back against a defender chasing that action.

Trap and Wham are available against an interior defender who is getting upfield. Toss/Crack and Pin-Pull get blockers outside when he likes the edge matchup. Jet sweep is a real handoff, and the same motion can accompany an inside run or play-action pass. Draw is available when the front is rushing the passer aggressively. Those calls come into the weekly plan when the opponent gives him a reason to use them; he does not need every run in the book on every call sheet.

He generally likes a committee of backs with overlapping skills. He can emphasize one on outside-zone runs, another behind pullers and his best protector in passing situations, while continuing to teach each the broader offense. A back who is running well can stay in. A substitution should not automatically tell the defense whether the next call is a run, screen or dropback pass. Protection, ball security, health and the game situation affect his choices alongside the runner's production.

## Defense

Stone builds from a 4-3 Under front, with the three-technique on the weak side and the nose shaded to the strength. He can move the three-technique to the strong side in Over, spread the front in Nickel Even, cover the interior linemen with Bear or use an Odd front to change the center's blocking rules. The front he chooses depends on the run surface, the protection and which blocker he wants to put into a difficult matchup.

He carries a true three-linebacker base defense, nickel and selected three-safety packages. Against a receiving tight end, a third safety may give him a better matchup while retaining a run defender. Against a heavier group, he can add a linebacker or reduce the front. He also checks what happens when those tight ends detach or the back leaves the backfield. Adding size is useful only if the resulting group can cover the eligible receivers. A safety who can play the post is not automatically his best choice to cover a slot receiver or fit against a pulling guard.

### Pass defense

Stone prefers to get pressure with four and keep seven in coverage. He likes tackle-end games, changes in alignment and interior rushers who can force the quarterback off his spot. He studies how the tackle and guard pass off a stunt and which blocker loses when left alone. Against a mobile quarterback, the ends' rush depth and inside pursuit matter: the pressure has to leave someone responsible for the escape lane. A spy is available when the quarterback warrants it.

He likes man coverage with help placed where the offense wants to throw. Cover 1 Robber gives him a deep safety and an underneath defender working the dig and crossing windows. The man defenders use their leverage to direct receivers toward that help. Against bunches and stacks, he wants the exchange decided before the snap so two defenders do not follow the same release.

Cover 3 is a frequent zone answer, with Buzz and Cloud rotations changing the underneath support and outside leverage. He also carries Quarters, Cover 2, Tampa, Palms and split-field coverage such as Cover 6. Those calls let him change how vertical routes are distributed and where the safety help arrives. A two-high alignment can stay split or rotate into Cover 3 or man coverage. The underneath defenders still have to sort the releases, particularly a shallow crosser with a seam behind it.

Brackets give him a way to double a receiver or take away a route combination. He wants to identify the actual problem before committing the extra defender and know which other receiver or area becomes harder to cover. He can change the help without replacing the whole coverage.

When he sends five, he wants a protection reason. An edge pressure can make the back widen; an A-gap pressure can test the center and back's exchange; a slot pressure can attack a formation tendency. Fire Zone gives him three deep defenders and three underneath behind the rush. He identifies the likely hot throw and the defender who must close on it. He will also show pressure and rush four, or replace an edge rusher with a linebacker or defensive back. The replacement rush is a changeup within the defense.

Screens receive their own work. A rusher being let through must recognize the releasing blockers and retrace. The inside pursuit overlaps toward the back. On perimeter throws, the corner calls the crack and the next support defender replaces the player being blocked. Stone does not want everyone widening toward a possible bubble while the offense runs through the space they have left.

### Run defense

Stone wants each defender to know his gap and where his help is coming from. In the Under front, the weak three-technique takes the B-gap and the nose controls the strong A-gap; the linebackers fit the spaces assigned behind them. A slant or pressure can change those jobs, but someone has to replace the defender leaving his gap. Penetration gets judged by the runner's path. Getting into the backfield while the ball escapes through the vacated gap earns a correction.

Against inside zone, he watches the combination blocks, the lineman climbing to the linebacker and the backside cutback. Against outside zone, the edge must keep the runner from getting outside without opening a clean cutback. The backside edge and pursuit cannot both chase the front side. One still has the boot or quarterback keep.

Against Power and Counter, he follows the pullers. A box call keeps outside leverage and turns the runner into inside help. A spill call attacks the kick-out and sends the ball toward support outside. If the offense logs that defender, the next man overlaps outside; the wrapping puller becomes the linebacker's block to handle. Against split zone, the backside defender must recognize the slicing blocker and retain the assigned boot, squeeze or exchange responsibility.

Motion and extra blockers change the gap count. Stone wants the defense to name the new force player when an overhang travels with a receiver, and the extra fitter when another tight end or lineman joins the surface. Against a read or option, the handoff, quarterback and perimeter throw have separate owners. If the offense speeds up, he trims the movement and disguise before allowing those assignments to become unclear.

## Teaching and practice

Stone will stop a film session over a vague answer. A player saying there was a communication problem leaves him asking who made the call, what the other man heard and which assignment each played. He has time for the player who admits he did not understand it. The one who agrees with every correction and comes back unprepared gets much less patience. A coach who gave conflicting instructions has to answer for those too.

He teaches the whole concept, then the player's job within it. On Drive, someone clears the coverage, someone runs the dig and someone crosses underneath. Moving a tight end into the slot can change who carries a job without changing what the quarterback is reading. Stone gets into the release, the route depth and where the receiver needs to arrive as the quarterback reaches that part of the progression. On third-and-five, a route intended to break beyond the sticks cannot settle at four yards because the receiver met contact.

The film stops before the snap. The player identifies the front or coverage clue and states his assignment. The coach runs the play, finds the mistake and returns to where the answer changed. They compare a correct rep with the failed one, walk through the correction and take it onto the practice field. Stone distinguishes a player who never understood the assignment from one who understood it and used poor technique. A bad decision and a lack of effort bring different conversations again.

<a id="how-he-wants-training-camp-run"></a>

He likes a hard camp. Periods move quickly, players repeat the work and sustained drives can run ten or twelve snaps. He watches whether substitutions, alignments and calls stay together late in the drive. Players and assistants can still ask him questions. The correction needs to be short enough to use on the next rep.

Individual periods work into group periods and team. Receivers repeat releases and coverage adjustments. Backs work the track of the run, their protection assignments and their routes. Inside run gives the line and backs work together; passing periods have to join the quarterback's feet to the receivers' timing. Seven-on-seven is useful to him, but he needs a rush present to examine protection.

After a failed combination block, the coach resets the front and identifies the linebacker. The two linemen walk through the exchange. They repeat it at the period's tempo. They run the combination again in team work. Later, the staff checks it without supplying the answer first.

Stone moves starters and reserves through different groups so a reserve gets useful repetitions and individual feedback. He makes room for backed-up, red-zone, two-minute and four-minute work. When a correction keeps failing, he and the position coach change the teaching setting or the assignment.

He starts a developing player with a job he can carry. A receiving tight end can work from the slot or wing while his attached blocking improves. A powerful runner can contribute on early downs while learning protection. Stone asks which routes the player trusts, which blocks feel natural and where he hesitates. He and the position coach use those answers to choose the work, then watch whether the player can execute it without another reminder.

Players and coaches, including Stone, meet written physical standards suited to their duties and health. A failed test leads to an individual plan and a retest. Medical restrictions and practice limits remain in force.

## Calling the game

Stone prepares roughly thirty opening calls. They let him see how the defense handles his formations and personnel, but a short-yardage down or a backed-up possession takes him to that part of the sheet. He can come back to an unused opening call later.

Between possessions, he talks through the last series with the quarterback and staff. A frustrated quarterback can tell him which throws he is seeing and which calls he trusts. Stone wants to know whether the defense took away the route, the quarterback got there late or the protection broke before the throw developed.

A defender sitting in the dig window can leave the shallow answer open. A rounded break needs work with the receiver's coach. If the quarterback cannot get to the throw before the rush arrives, Stone can shorten the throw or add protection. He will go to quick game, a screen or another run emphasis, but the change comes from football the players have practiced. A route adjustment uses the tags and landmarks the quarterback and receiver share.

He keeps looking for first downs and points when he has a lead. If the defense loads up against the run, a practiced cross, boot or quick conversion can stay on the sheet. Before a four-minute possession, he goes over the covered throw, sack risk, remaining timeouts and snap timing with the quarterback. As the clock runs down, keeping the ball inbounds and making the defense use a timeout can take priority. He still wants the first down that keeps his offense on the field.

In two-minute offense, he prefers to keep the desired personnel on the field. The players get the ball back to the official, confirm the formation signal and get the next call. Before the snap, the quarterback needs to know whether a throw in the middle leaves time to line up again or requires a timeout. After a long gain, the next formation and call need to reach the players while they get downfield. Stone has an assistant supplying timeout, fourth-down and challenge information while he calls plays; the decision stays with him.

His defense becomes more cautious with a fourth-quarter lead. He leans toward protecting against the deep ball, allowing completions in front and making tackles inbounds. With a narrow lead, the short gains he concedes can carry the opponent toward field-goal range. In a tie, he is more inclined to pressure the quarterback and seek a disruptive play.

## Special teams

Stone gives the kicking game periods on the practice schedule and time in meetings. Coverage and protection can earn a reserve a place among the active players. He reviews the missed block in punt protection, the coverage lane that opened, the return decision and the penalty that changed field position. The coordinator handles the technical coaching and the week's plan.

The field-goal, punt, fourth-down and fake decisions stay with Stone. He wants the choice communicated before players start coming on and off the field. He enjoys a prepared fake. The opponent has to give him a look worth attacking, and he wants to see his own players execute it in preparation before he authorizes it.

## Assistants and personnel

Stone wants assistants who can teach their positions. A line coach needs to explain why a protection failed and bring a correction. A position coach needs to tell him when a player's development has stalled. He gives them room to choose drills and teaching examples, argue for a player and tell him the install is too large. Coordinators have units to run and can disagree with his plan.

Changing a receiver's adjustment affects the quarterback. Changing the way a lineman passes off pressure can affect the back. Stone expects the coaches involved to settle that work before they give the players competing instructions. He tells assistants he will let them finish their corrections. He cuts into an offensive correction before the position coach has finished.

His personnel requests describe the jobs he needs filled. For an attached tight end, he specifies the edge blocks, combinations and routes, which of them the player must perform immediately and which the staff can teach. A player from another team's cuts can fit that request. So can a low-cost signing or a draft pick. Stone is comfortable with the general manager holding final acquisition, contract and cap authority.

He can see a useful role in a prospect whose missing skills concern the personnel staff. He has a way he would start the player and a part he thinks he can teach. The general manager may judge the time and roster place differently. Stone argues his evaluation and coaches the roster that results.

## Players, families and the press

Stone is measured and articulate in public, with humor when it fits. When the team plays badly, he answers for his call, the preparation or the situation he put the players in. He will blame his call when he knows a player missed the assignment. In the meeting, he goes through the player's actual mistake with the same precision he would after a win. Repeated carelessness brings a private disciplinary conversation and can cost playing time.

He gives credit for a good block, a reserve's preparation or an assistant's adjustment even after a loss. He can discuss missed tackles and failed protection at the press conference without singling out a player for blame. The individual correction stays in the building.

His contact with players and their families continues through injuries and poor stretches. There are meals and conversations when a player is losing playing time too. Stone explains a reduced role directly. Knowing him well does not preserve a player's snaps or roster place, and the relationship can remain strained afterward.

A player can keep parts of his life private. Stone wants medical help reachable at any hour and access to multiple qualified psychologists; he encourages players to use them. Clinicians decide treatment, clearance and restrictions. He also wants a family liaison whom spouses and partners can approach about problems at home, including abuse by a player. Reports involving safety go to qualified help and the appropriate reporting process. He will not suppress a report to protect an important player or try to settle it over dinner.

## Current Jacksonville application

Through September 28, 2014: Stone calls the offense; Mike Tice coordinates its weekly work. Romeo Crennel called the defense through Week 3. Stone's September 21 instruction transferred defensive calling to him from Week 4, with Crennel continuing to coordinate preparation and transmit calls. Mike Westhoff runs special teams. Dave Caldwell retains final acquisition, contract and cap authority. The Week 4 backfield is an instructed hierarchy of Maurice Jones-Drew, C.J. Anderson and Jonathan Grimes. These are dated assignments and decisions alongside Stone's broader preferences.
