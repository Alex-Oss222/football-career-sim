# Head-coach media sessions: how real ones run and how Stone's are written

Prepared October 1, 2026 from the user's instruction that the pregame and postgame media sections must read like a real, specific person answering real reporters, with Stone's [coaching profile](../career/coaching_profiles/alex_stone_coaching_profile.md) as the reference. It governs section 3 and section 5 of the [preseason output](../foundation/templates/preseason_output_template.md) and section 2 and section 4 of the [regular-season output](../foundation/templates/regular_season_output_template.md). It is a writing method. It supplies no event, no quote and no fact; the branch supplies those.

The research below was done against real transcripts read in full. The verbatim excerpts are short and attributed; they show how a coach handles a kind of question and are never copied into Stone's mouth. Later-dated sessions inform the form only. Nothing a real coach said about a real player becomes evidence about anyone in this career.

## 1. How real sessions run

Verbatim question-and-answer transcripts (ASAP Sports, 2025) are marked **Q&A**; club transcripts that paraphrase each question as a label and quote the answer in full (Atlanta, 2010 to 2014) are marked **label**.

Note a refused host in the week's engine note rather than inventing a session from memory of it.

**The opening.** A head coach may or may not open with a statement. Payton walked in mid-sentence and closed with "Any questions?". Daboll opened with "All right. What do ya got, guys?" after one loss and, a week later, with a two-paragraph statement that led with the quarterback's concussion and the fourth-quarter lead that was lost. Smith opened every postgame session with a statement that named the phases that failed or worked, then "with that, I'll open it up for questions." Preseason statements run longer and cover roster business: Smith on August 28, 2014 talked through the 53-man deadline and thanked the players about to be cut. Stone's opening, when he gives one, is one short paragraph on the game; after a loss it starts with him.

**Question order.** The first questions go to the largest thing in the game: the defense after three straight games of 30 points allowed, the quarterback's injury, the comeback. Then the room moves through the specific plays (a fourth-down decision, a blocked kick, one coverage), the individual players (the backup quarterback, a rookie tackle, a guard returning), and the injuries. The last questions are the wide ones: the record, the job, the message to the locker room, the bye week. A session is not grouped by topic; a question about the kicker can sit between one about the quarterback and one about the defense because a different reporter asked it.

**Follow-ups.** The same reporter presses when the first answer was short. Daboll: "I just say it's unfortunate he got hurt." Next question, from the room: "Brian, is this sustainable, the way he's playing?" Then: "What do you mean by that?" Then, after a no-microphone question: "I don't know the play. I don't know when it was." Four questions on one subject is normal after a loss with an injury. After a win the follow-up is friendlier and still specific: "Did you get a chance to talk afterwards?" "Yeah, just real quickly." "How is he?"

**How beat writers phrase it.** Real questions are rarely clean. They are clipped ("What did you think of Tyler?", "What was working about the run game?"), they trail off and the coach starts answering ("You had the blocked field goal, obviously --"), they carry the reporter's own count or stat ("Three straight games with 30-plus points", "Tatum had 16 total tackles today", "I think you outscored them 26-0 in the fourth quarter"), they are two questions in one ("Two questions. What did he mean to you? How was it to get that win for him?", "Coach, two-part question. First... Also..."), they are statements ("He looked pretty beat up after the game, Jaxson."), they contain the reporter's premise ("I know you don't want to dampen his competitiveness and you love his fire and all that stuff. He's not 250 pounds."), and some are inaudible on the recording and the transcript says so. A pregame conference call is calmer and more preparatory: "On preparing to play the Falcons", "On the status Chris Gamble", "On Chris Gamble's participation in Wednesday's practice".

**Length.** Postgame sessions after a routine win run 15 to 25 questions. After a loss with an injury or a decision in dispute they run as long and press harder, with more one-sentence answers. A preseason session is shorter and the answers are about evaluation, roster deadlines and who played with whom. A midweek call is eight to twelve questions, most about the opponent and availability.

## 2. How coaches actually answer

Each entry gives the shape, a verbatim excerpt and the source. The excerpts are models of a kind of answer, not lines for Stone.

**The straight answer.** A specific question gets a specific, short answer with the detail the coach already knows. "Fourth and four. So that was -- if it was a certain distance we would have went for it. Made it a ten-point game, I believe. That's the reason why." (Daboll, November 2, 2025.) "I thought about it. I thought take the points, get a ten-point lead in the fourth. Our defense was playing good." (Daboll, November 9, 2025.) "He was inactive last week with a hamstring injury... He was inactive for the game because of the hamstring injury." (Fox, December 8, 2010.)

**The deflection to the tape, once.** The coach says he has not seen it and names what he will look at. "Yeah, we'll look at that on film. All of that without watching it on tape. My concern was just the late subs. It was a little bit sloppy." (Payton.) "I didn't get a great angle of it so obviously big-time play." (Moore.) "I know he got that one penalty, but from what I saw, I disagreed with it. So we'll see when we see the tape." (Shanahan.) The real coach uses it once or twice a session, and attaches a specific observation to it each time. He never uses it to avoid a question he can answer.

**The protection of a player.** The coach talks about the opponent's design, the play, or the whole unit, and keeps the individual's error inside. "They had a nice play design and guessed what had we were playing and ran across from the opposite side and linebacker got picked. Give them credit." (Daboll, November 2.) "I think he battles. Kid gives everything he has. Competes 60 minutes. Makes good decisions. You've got to do a good job of finishing plays around him, too." (Daboll on his rookie quarterback after a loss.) "He made a mistake. We brought him off the field, and then he had to go back on. I said, 'Now, calm down. You have to go out there, forget it, next play.'" (Bradley on a safety's penalty, August 14, 2014.) Smith on a cornerback's absence: "Injury related... it was not performance-related at all, it was injury related." (November 18, 2013.)

**The admission of a bad call.** It is short and it is first-person. "Was George supposed to get the jet sweep on that?" "Yeah, he was, yeah. Pretty bad call, though." (Shanahan.) "When we start talking about play calling, there is only one person responsible for play calling and that is me, and we haven't gotten it done." (Smith, November 18, 2013.) "One of them being a team that is not being coached as well as they need to be coached, and I will take full responsibility for that." (Smith, same session.) "Look, none of us did a good enough job. That starts with me." (Daboll, November 2.)

**The dry one-liner.** "How many games you've scored a touchdown in the opening drive, what are you seeing in that opening drive that's giving you guys such success?" "Good execution." (Daboll.) "Sean, we've reached the half way point in the season..." "It's impossible to have the halfway point because we're on odd number of games. We passed the halfway point. Yeah, all right. Go ahead. I'm kidding you." Then: "How do you assess the progress of your team?" "It is what it is. What's our record? I lost track." "7-2." "There you go. That's how I see it. That's pretty good." (Payton.) "Is there a possibility that he'll need surgery after the season?" "Not that I've been told." (Shanahan.) The one-liner is an answer, not a refusal, and it is never aimed at the reporter.

**The refusal on injuries.** The coach gives the fact he has (the player left, was evaluated, will be reassessed) and nothing he does not. "No, I don't. I know I saw him after the ball game and he seems to be fine... And that's really all I know. I know that he was not going to be able to come back in the ball game." (Smith, October 17, 2010.) "Yeah. Yeah, it's unfortunate but, you know, unfortunate he got hurt." (Daboll, when asked whether the hits caught up to his quarterback.) "I'm not sure exactly of the play that it happened. No, I'm concerned for the kid." (Daboll.) "We'll reassess it the same way we did this week. Wasn't that close to doing it." (Shanahan on his injured starter.) Fox, asked whether a cornerback practised: "We have already practiced today. He did not practice."

**"I'll have to look at it."** Distinct from the tape deflection: a decision the coach made and has not reviewed. "I got to look at it to see if we weren't able to get the points you want. I got to look at seeing where we can improve there. Execution-wise, schematically, get ourselves in position maybe. I'll look at how those time-outs progressed." (Moore on his end-of-half clock.) "You'll need to look at those --" "I will." (Payton.)

**The credit to an assistant or a teammate.** Named, specific, and often unprompted. "I thought Vance and those guys did a good job with the plan on how much snaps Greenlaw was going to play. How we were going to use Barron." (Payton.) "I thought coach (Dirk) Koetter and coach (Glenn) Thomas did a good job, along with Matt Ryan, in getting him prepared." (Smith, August 28, 2014.) "The coaching staff put together a very good plan. Mike (Nolan) and his staff did a great job." (Smith, December 21, 2014.) "I thought Shane tried to mix it a number of times these last couple drives; play zone, pressure, play man." (Daboll, November 9, defending his coordinator after a collapse.) "I can't say enough about Tae. He's playing the toughest position in football, left tackle." (Ryans on a rookie.)

**The non-answer to a hypothetical or a job question.** The coach declines the frame and returns to the one thing he will talk about. "Brian, are you worried about your job?" "I'm just focused on these guys in the locker room." (Daboll.) "6-3 instead of 5-4, does it change your approach at the trade deadline?" "Yeah, I think it's been the exact same, regardless of our record... Nothing changes based off today." (Shanahan.) "I appreciate Mr. Blank's comments, Thomas and Rich, but really my focus is on one thing and that is preparing and having our team ready to play against the New Orleans Saints." (Smith.) "I can't speak for what's in their minds. Again, division games are what they are." (Fox.) "Do you think this game meant something more to Chris...?" "I think that's fair to ask CO." (Moore.)

**The quarterback question.** The coach assesses the quarterback in the quarterback's own terms (decisions, ball security, rhythm, where the ball went) and names the help he needed. "I thought Mac did a hell of a job, extremely efficient in what he was doing, going against a real tough pass rush. Probably only one negative play in the game, just the fumble there at the end of the second quarter... took care of the ball, got the ball to the right guys, and made a couple plays with his legs, too." (Shanahan.) "What did you think of Tyler?" "Yeah, I thought Tyler took an awesome step today. I thought he made some big-time plays, some off-schedule plays to the Juwan, off-schedule, moving within the pocket." (Moore.) Payton, asked about his quarterback passing up short gains: "Listen, we don't talk about that... you got to be careful you don't tell that player too much when it comes to something like that." Smith in preseason gives the line and what it means for the decision: "I believe he only had five incompletions in 20 attempts and 243 yards with a 148.5 quarterback rating. To have a quarterback play like that you have to feel good because the number two is a good player as well."

**The preseason answer.** It is about evaluation and the roster, and it says what the game was for. "We came into the game tonight wanting to take a good look at a lot of guys. I thought that we got an opportunity to do that tonight." (Smith, September 2, 2010.) "The preseason is for evaluating players, but it's also for working on new things systematically that you want to integrate." (Same.) "We're just trying to find the best combination. We're pleased with Mike Brewster, but we want to see other guys just to have some versatility." (Bradley on rotating centers, August 14, 2014.) On the starters' snap count: "the move was made because the first-team offense had run 30 plays, which was around the ideal number for that unit." (Bradley, same.)

**The answer that corrects the reporter's premise, without heat.** "Are those tears of emotion or is that just --" "Whose?" "Yours." "No, it wasn't tears." (Daboll.) "Sorry about that." "Yeah, that's okay." (Same session, after a reporter's remark about the quarterback.)

## 3. Anti-slop rules for answers

These are the ways generated press conferences fail, with the real-transcript evidence for the rule.

1. **No answer restates the question.** A real coach starts on the substance: "Fourth and four." "Good execution." "Injury related." Never "That's a good question about the fourth-down decision. The thought process there was..."
2. **No three-part lists built for rhythm.** Real answers list what the coach is actually counting ("four or five, six, average 4.1, 40 carries") and stop. A sentence shaped "it's about preparation, execution and finishing" is slop unless the coach is quoting a team principle the branch already holds.
3. **Banned phrases**, because the fourteen real sessions do not contain them: "at the end of the day", "it is what it is" as a closer (Payton used it once as a joke about the record; it is his, not a formula), "we'll take it one game at a time" unless the branch's Stone has an established reason, "control what we can control", "credit to them" as a sentence on its own, "we'll go back and look at the film" more than once a session, "I'm not going to get into that" more than once a session, "that's football", "next man up" as a complete answer (Smith used it inside a specific sentence about the backup), "we'll be fine".
4. **No sermon.** A coach after a loss speaks in short declaratives and does not deliver a paragraph on character, culture or the standard. Ryans's post-comeback answers are the longest in the set and they are about one game, one quarterback and one line; even those run five sentences, not fifteen.
5. **No explaining the plan's rationale unprompted.** A real coach explains a decision when asked ("What was the thought process on kicking the field goal") and otherwise names the result. Stone's priorities belong in section 2 of the output, not in his podium answers, unless a question draws one out.
6. **No coach-speak the transcripts do not show.** The real vocabulary is plain: "ran it", "got picked", "didn't get him down", "out of their lanes", "the ball should never get over our heads", "took care of the ball", "got the ball to the right guys", "the late subs", "a full slide on one of the sacks". Not "executed at a high level", "won the leverage battle", "attacked the matchup", "stayed within ourselves", "played complementary football" (Ryans and Moore each said it once about a specific half; it is not a filler).
7. **Sentence length and interruptions as real coaches produce them.** Answers are built of short sentences with a longer one in the middle, and they break: "Yeah, and look, they ran it. I wouldn't say they had a ton of explosives but they ran it and they were pretty productive running the football." "I'm not sure exactly of the play that it happened. No, I'm concerned for the kid." A coach can stop mid-thought ("So --") and the next question comes. Fragments are allowed. Written answers do not need to be grammatical paragraphs.
8. **One sentence when a real coach would give one.** "How is he?" "He's just down." "Will Russell start next week if Jaxson can't?" "Yeah, we'll see." "What was working about the run game?" "Physicality, Alvin, credit to him." A third of the answers in a postgame session after a loss are one or two sentences. Give them that length.
9. **One subject per answer.** The coach answers the question that was asked and does not append the other things he wanted to say. If he wants to credit the line, a reporter asks about the line, or he does it in his opening.
10. **The reporter is not the enemy and not a friend.** No "Great question." No sarcasm at the room. Dry is fine; scornful is not in any transcript read here.
11. **Numbers only as the coach would have them at the podium.** He knows the score, the down and distance of a decision, a sack count he was told, a quarterback's line if the sheet was handed to him (Smith quoted it in preseason; Payton asked the room for the record). He does not know snap percentages, net punting average or a defender's pass-defended total ten minutes after the game.
12. **The injury answer gives what is known and stops.** Left the game, evaluated, reassessed on a date, the doctors' call. No prognosis the medical owner has not issued, no "we think he'll be fine".

## 4. Stone's voice sheet

Derived from the [coaching profile](../career/coaching_profiles/alex_stone_coaching_profile.md) (sections cited by number), the [NFL coach sheet](../career/coaching_profiles/alex_stone_nfl_coach_sheet.md) and the media passages of [The Prowl](../career/2013/offseason/the_prowl_program_identity.md#media-voice). The profile is the reference; where this sheet and a later profile revision differ, the profile controls.

**What he answers straight.** His own decisions: the exit rule for a quarterback's series, the fourth-down call, the punt, who started and why, what the plan was for a group (profile §7, §9, §13; The Prowl, "After a Loss"). He gives the down, the distance and the reasoning in two or three sentences. He answers a technical question about an assignment with the actual job ("the point was declared to the left, the back scans right") when the branch record holds that fact, because he wants players and reporters to understand the offense (§1, §11). He says what a game was for before the game and holds to it afterward (§3, §8).

**What he protects.** The individual player's error, including after an obvious mistake: he names the play, the correction stays inside, and he will blame the call when he knows the player missed the assignment (§12; The Prowl, "After a Player Makes a Costly Mistake"). Internal discipline, unless availability changes (§2, §12; The Prowl, "Public Discipline"). Medical detail beyond what the clinicians have issued (§14). A player's private life (§14). The roster: acquisitions, releases, contracts and the 53 are Caldwell's and Stone says so in one clause (§10).

**Where he takes blame.** First, and before anything else, after a loss: the plan he approved, the call he made, the situation he put a player in (§12; The Prowl, "After a Loss"). He also takes it for a sideline operation fault (a call that left its zone, a script not run in order) because the sideline is his (§4, §7). He does not take it theatrically or at length; one sentence, then the football reason.

**When he is dry.** When a question asks him to sell something ("is this a statement?", "do you believe in this group now?"), when a question contains its own answer, when he is asked to grade a competition after one game, and when the room asks him to be angry at a kicker or a rookie. Humor when it fits, never at a player (§12; The Prowl: "Dry when appropriate"). He does not perform frustration.

**How he credits.** Specifically and by name, including after a loss and including work away from the ball: a block held, a reserve's preparation, a coordinator's adjustment, a position coach's correction that showed up (§2, §12). He credits the opponent's design plainly when it beat him. He does not credit "the guys" in the abstract.

**The injury question.** He says what the medical record says and no more: removed, in the protocol or on a hold, reassessed on a date, the trainers or doctors decide (§14, The Prowl's readiness standard). He will not put a number of weeks on it himself. He will say whether a player's absence is his decision or the medical staff's, because that distinction is public football availability (The Prowl, "Public Discipline").

**The quarterback question.** He assesses the quarterback by the things he coaches: the read, the timing, the decision, ball security, command of the operation, what the protection and the routes gave him (§5, §7, §11). He separates a covered route from a late throw from a protection failure (§7). He will say a throw was a bad decision without turning it into a verdict on the player, and he reviews successful plays that came from poor decisions too (§8). He does not announce a depth-chart result from the podium after one game (§8; Document 3 authority).

**The kicker question.** The operation before the kicker: snap, hold, protection, then the kick (§13). He gives it to the special-teams coordinator by name and says what would change his mind. He does not pile on a specialist in public and he does not pretend a miss did not cost the game.

**The roster question.** "Anything about the roster is Dave's." He will describe the football need he has brought to Caldwell in the terms he uses inside (§10: the edge blocks, the combinations, the routes a player must carry) only when it is already public; he will not pre-announce a decision, a cut or a signing.

**The "is the job decided?" question.** Not from one game (§8; the position-battle rule). He says what the game added as evidence and what remains untested, and he says when the decision will be made and by whom. He does not manufacture a competition where there is none and does not leave a settled starter hanging to create news.

**Things Stone would never say at a podium.**

- "The Prowl" as a slogan more than once in a season's worth of sessions (The Prowl, "Media Voice").
- A character judgment about a player after a mistake, or the word "accountability" aimed at one man (§12).
- A player's medical prognosis, treatment or restriction that the clinicians have not issued (§14).
- A roster, contract or cap statement that belongs to Caldwell (§10).
- "I'm not going to answer that" as a tone; he declines by stating what he will talk about instead.
- A claim to have seen film he has not seen, or a cause for a play the record does not hold (§2: vague answers stop the meeting; the same standard applies to him).
- A promise about next week's result, a lineup he has not set, or a hypothetical ("if we had gone for it we score").
- Anything coached through the press at a player ("he needs to know that...") (The Prowl: does not use the podium to coach through a player).
- A complaint about officiating as the reason for a result.
- A rating, a grade or a number for a player.

## 5. The question-writing rule

1. Take each question from a real transcript, as the reporter asked it. Keep the wording, the length, the premise, the count the reporter brought, the two-in-one, the trailing-off and the statement-question. Change only the names, the numbers and the game details that belong to the branch. Do not write a question from scratch and do not tidy a real one.
2. Prefer **Q&A** transcripts (verbatim questions). Where only a **label** transcript exists ("On if he knew anything about Robinson's condition"), the adapted question keeps the label's substance and is phrased as a reporter would phrase it; say so in the source line.
3. Choose the real question whose situation matches the branch's: a quarterback injured, a backup starting, a kicker's misses, a punt in a tie, a rookie's first start, a preseason rotation. The match is the situation, never the real result.
4. Run the questions in the order a real session runs: the largest thing first, plays and players in the middle, the wide questions last. Let the same subject return when a real room would return to it.
5. Every question gets a source line: the coach who was asked it, the club, the date and the transcript link. The source line is for the record; the question in the output carries no citation.
6. The answer is Stone's and contains only what the branch record supports. The podium may hold back what the staff knows; it may never contradict the branch or claim an observation the record does not hold.
7. The session's length follows the game. A routine preseason or regular-season win sits at the low end of the template's range; a loss, an injury to a starter or a disputed decision pushes it up and the follow-ups get shorter and sharper.

## 6. Worked example: the Chicago overtime loss, postgame

A rewrite of section 5 of [preseason game 2](../career/2014/04_Training_Camp_and_Preseason/Preseason_Games/Game_02/output.md) (Chicago 23, Jacksonville 17, overtime, August 14, 2014) in this method. It uses only facts already in that output's game section and report. It is an example of the method; the game output itself is unchanged.

**Stone:** Another one that got away late. Kirk's group did what we asked, the young groups moved it, and we missed three kicks and gave up one throw in overtime. Start with me on the punt at the end. Go ahead.

**Reporter:** What do you see with your kicking game right now? Three misses, two from 43.

**Stone:** Four operations, two snappers, one make from 53 and three misses. I do not have a snap or a hold or a protection fault on any of the four right now. Mike will go through all four tomorrow before I say it is Josh.

**Reporter:** You'll need to look at those --

**Stone:** I will.

**Reporter:** What was the thought process on punting from the 41 with a minute and change left?

**Stone:** Fourth-and-5. A 59-yard try after two misses from 43 was not a kick I was going to ask for. So I kicked it out of bounds at the 10 and gave the ball to the defense, and they got it back for us with nine seconds. I will look at it. In a game I said was for evaluation, that was a fourth-down rep I did not give that group, and that is mine.

**Reporter:** What did you think of Kirk's performance, the touchdown to Lewis, the pick, his progression at all?

**Stone:** He operated. Two series, both got to their 33 or better, one ended in seven and one ended in a pick. The third-and-10 to Lewis and the third-and-3 to Thielen were the throws I wanted to see. The interception was a first-down Snag call from the 33, a low-red-zone call that left its zone. That is on my sheet before it is on Kirk. I need to see the route and the read before I split it any further.

**Reporter:** Was Hakeem supposed to get the ball on that one?

**Stone:** He was the target. Whether the throw should have gone is the question.

**Reporter:** Alex, what is happening on these long ones? It just seems like there's too many of these.

**Stone:** Tampa was three against the first group. Tonight was one, in overtime, against the last group, their third quarterback. That group had just held them to minus six and sacked him three times. I do not know yet whether it was the call, the help or a throw that beat sound coverage, and I am not putting a name on it from the sideline. Romeo will have it tomorrow afternoon.

**Reporter:** How did you feel like Cornelius Lucas did at right tackle?
