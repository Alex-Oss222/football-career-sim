# Writing a game turn

Adopted October 18, 2026 to change how future game turns are written, and revised October 20, 2026 for game week version 2 ([docs/game_week_v2.md](game_week_v2.md), section 6, uploaded and adopted by the user). This file is the one owner of the prose guidance for a Game Report. The [head-coach media method](head_coach_media_method.md) is the one owner of its media guidance. The [Game Report template](../foundation/templates/game_week/game_report_template.md) owns the section order and what goes where, and [docs/run_week.md](run_week.md) owns the steps that fill and check it.

**Which page.** From 2014 Week 6 every played game, regular season and postseason, has one result page: its Game Report (`Game_Report.md` in the game's folder, `runtime.seasons.SeasonPaths.game_report`), the last of the game week's three pages after the Game Week Briefing and the Game Plan. Its layout is Coach record, sections 1 to 7, then Full game statistics, with the complete generated box score last. 2014 Weeks 1 to 5 keep their `output.md` as written, in the layout of the [regular-season output template](../foundation/templates/regular_season_output_template.md); a request to revise this process or a template does not authorize rewriting them. The [preseason template](../foundation/templates/preseason_output_template.md) keeps its own eight sections; sections 1 to 5 below apply to it where its template does not say otherwise.

The earlier instructions encouraged writing one field at a time, covering the same statistics in several sections, borrowing transcript questions and forcing answer shapes, and they put the complete statistics and a 53-man availability inventory inside the story. That made a technically complete report sound assembled and buried the game. The workflow below replaces those instructions. Mechanical validation checks the record; a separate editorial review checks whether the account and the conversation work.

## 1. Establish the evidence and the scope

Read the applicable instructions, the current season state, the approved preparation and decisions (the Game Week Briefing as issued and the Game Plan as applied), the closed game's record and its public play-by-play, the medical changes and any part of the report already written. Use the actual season paths and the active playbook. For a rewrite, work from the closed event: do not redraw, advance time, revise a user decision or add an activity that was never recorded.

Identify the football facts that matter, the authorized decisions and any material unknowns. Retain preparation dates, recorded practice work, the user's wording and the supporting sources. A style revision may reorganize that evidence; it must not silently turn documented work into an omission or use a plausible weekly schedule to invent missing work.

Sections 1 and 2 (the preparation and the pregame media) are written from what was known before kickoff and are frozen before the game, as the opened report the practice week's close commits. If the rest is written after the result, nothing in those sections is backfilled: no prediction, concern, question or coaching intention because it fits the result.

Keep the event-record lines and the box-score markers intact. The box score is generated from the closed game by the renderer, last on the page; the coach record table is printed by the coach-record tool. No script fills narrative paragraphs, coaching explanations or dialogue by reciting fields, and none of those tools authors the football account or approves its claims.

Distinguish a recorded play, an attributed staff observation, an authorized decision and an inference before drafting. Read the active record's attribution rules before using a field as evidence of a player's skill or a coach's choice: the [runtime documentation](../runtime/README.md) controls that distinction. A call name attached to a played snap may be a compatible label matched after the play, and the defensive sheet is not an input to the outcome, so a label is not proof of the tactical reason for a call. A timeout total does not establish a coach's choice or its reason. A statistical credit can be valid for the box score without supporting a technique or development judgment. Missing authoring data does not establish that a coach failed to observe something. Keep these distinctions in the working review rather than turning them into repeated prose disclaimers.

## 2. Decide what the report needs to explain

Before filling the template, identify the sequences that decided the game, the supported coaching findings, the decisions that mattered and any actual change to availability or roles. Choose the football substance first, then put it in its section. The presence of a prompt is never evidence that something happened.

| Part | What belongs there |
|---|---|
| Coach record | The generated record table as of the completed game. |
| 1. Preparation and final game-day decisions | The actual dated work, the plan in force (the one or two opponent-specific ideas that shaped the game, linked to the Game Plan), the final medical and inactive decisions and who made them, and the change from the previous game. Not a reprint of the briefing or the playbook; no generic NFL week in place of the recorded one. |
| 2. Pregame media | The documented session as it stood before kickoff ([media method](head_coach_media_method.md)), or the sentence that none was recorded. |
| 3. The game | The action in order, told through the particular sequences that explain the result. The lead says what happened and how it turned, with the names and sequences someone who watched would remember. Quiet quarters may be short. The calls and decisions that shaped the game get their own subsection when real events earn it. |
| 4. Postgame media | The session from what was known immediately after the game. |
| 5. Football review and next week's work | The meaningful finding for each unit and for game management, and the specific change for the next game with its coach and its next chance to be verified. A proposal stays a proposal. Do not retell the quarters. |
| 6. After-action report | Only what the game changed: new injuries, holds and clearances, the players whose work stood out, material role or transaction consequences. |
| 7. Final record and next game | The record, the standing or playoff result, the next scheduled event and the links to the official records. |
| Full game statistics | The complete generated game book, last. |

**The story.** Tell the action in order and use particular sequences, not one mechanical paragraph per drive. Do not declare emotional turning points the record does not show, and do not open with a cliché about momentum, resilience or execution. A story may report that Jones-Drew gained 15 on a third-and-16 screen, and separately note that the pregame plan permitted the screen against a credible rush; it may not claim the defense rushed six or the coach read a linebacker blitz without that evidence.

**A claimed reason for a call.** Before writing that a named play was chosen for a reason, answer three questions: was that call actually chosen before the snap, was its reason established then, and does the record contain the opponent's look? Where the answer is no, restrict the claim to what is recorded. A coach's error is specific when it is recorded: Week 5's field-goal decision was made before third down and stayed a field goal after the conversion left fourth-and-1 at the 2. An important game can have no confirmed schematic explanation for a particular turnover.

**Numbers.** The full statistics are preserved last; the prose does not repeat them. Select a number where it explains a sequence, and give a major number one home rather than repeating it across the lead, each quarter, the review and the press questions. There are no word quotas and no required equal length across offense, defense, special teams or players.

**Section 6 is an after-action report only.** New injuries, holds and clearances as the medical staff communicated them; the players whose work stood out, each with the play that earned it; ordered role changes, completed front-office moves and new role questions. An uneventful week has a terse statement ("No new injuries or availability changes were reported."). No 53-man inventory, no unchanged-group tables, no bench biographies, no status grades and no implementation notes. The roster and medical pages remain the owners of the current state.

**Win or loss.** The report explains the game to the same standard either way. There is no protagonist guarantee and no automatic praise or blame.

The same play may matter in more than one section when each use adds something: the story reports a failed conversion, the review explains a supported cause, and the carry-forward names the resulting work. Repeating the result, the numbers and the qualification in all three adds nothing. Omit empty optional findings. Do not force a controversy, an award, balanced praise and criticism, a question count, a paragraph count or a target length. State a shared evidence limit once, where it changes a conclusion.

## 3. The page is football only

A Game Report passes the clean-page check in repository validation (rebuild rule 4): no internal code or identifier, no file path, script or module name, no system note, disclaimer, research-source catalogue, generation or version label, and none of the words engine, branch, receipt, kernel, ledger, snapshot, checkpoint or simulation. Write "the game book", "the play-by-play", "the film", "the staff record". Link to pages (the Game Week Briefing, the Game Plan, the medical history, the standings, the statistics), never to a file in the data area; provenance stays in its technical owner, with at most the official-records links of section 7. The only markup is the event-record lines and the two box-score markers, each on a line of its own. Write preparation and game accounts in past tense, third person, with plain verbs and named people; no em dashes, rhetorical questions in the narrator's voice or one-line paragraphs for effect. Read the prose aloud and rewrite sentences that sound like a form being completed.

## 4. Use research where it helps

The [media research](head_coach_media_method.md#5-research-basis-and-limits) and [game-report research](preseason_game_reports.md) supply inspected references for form and football explanation. Use them without pretending to have researched them again. Read an additional source when an unfamiliar situation calls for it; research and verify a material rule or factual gap under the existing sourcebook requirements, such as the season's overtime rule for a playoff game. Do not borrow real results, diagnoses or player assessments to fill this career. No per-question citations, per-section source quotas or research-status lines appear on the page.

## 5. Review the draft before delivering it

Run a distinct editorial pass after drafting, using a separate reviewer when available. Read the report without the template prompts and without the Full game statistics first: can a reader identify the deciding possessions, the meaningful calls, an authentic media exchange and the next football issue? Return the affected passage for revision when:

- its content follows a field list instead of explaining a football matter, or several sections repeat the same account or the same numbers;
- a claimed cause of a play fails the three questions in section 2 above;
- a speaker explains software limitations, knows private or later information without a basis, or supplies a new decision or motive to make an answer work (the media method covers the dialogue in full);
- the prose claims a technical cause, a completed coaching action or an evaluation that its evidence does not establish;
- section 6 grows into an inventory.

Correct the passage and reread its section. Generic wording is repaired by finding the useful thought, not by replacing words. Then check the score and chronology, the names and participation, the information available at each session, the medical authority, the announced roles and the meaning of Stone's decisions against the sources. Every story moment must match the play-by-play, the clock, the score and the statistics: an eloquent but false play is a failure.

Finally use the repository and statistical checks ([docs/run_week.md](run_week.md)): the box score generated last and identical to the closed game; the header's final score, the line score, any overtime and the record agreeing with the game book (`runtime/game_report.py`); the clean-page check. A presentation-only edit retains the event identity, the result and the career date.

## 6. Keep the process consistent

This file owns the authoring sequence and the editorial review; the media method owns the dialogue guidance; the templates own section order and placement. AGENTS and the update workflow route here. When fixing a recurring writing problem, revise its controlling instruction here or in the media method and remove conflicting wording from the templates rather than appending another style mandate. Older reports and worked examples ([docs/examples/week_06_illustration.md](examples/week_06_illustration.md) among them) are evidence of their own events and illustrations of method, not a standard to imitate over these instructions.

For a template or process change, inspect the final diff: it must not change career outputs, event metadata, closed-game records, call sheets or current state. Review the prose guidance separately from the repository checks; a passing validator does not establish that the next report will read naturally. The editorial acceptance fixtures (`tests/fixtures/game_reports/`: a quiet game, a turnover-heavy game and a playoff game) show filled reports that pass every check.
